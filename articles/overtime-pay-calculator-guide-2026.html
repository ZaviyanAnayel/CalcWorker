FLSA Overtime Rules

Overtime Rate = Regular Rate x 1.5 (hours over 40/week)

$20/hr, 48 hours: Regular: 40 x 20 = $800. Overtime: 8 x 30 = $240. Total: $1,040.

Key rules:

  • Federal threshold: 40 hours/week (not 8/day - some states add daily OT).
  • Non-exempt employees must receive OT; exempt (salaried meeting duties/salary tests) need not.
  • 2026 salary threshold for exemption: check current DOL rules (recent updates raised it).

The "regular rate" includes far more than base hourly pay: non-discretionary bonuses, shift differentials, and commissions all inflate it. Worked example: $20/hour base plus a $2,000 quarterly production bonus. The bonus spreads over 13 weeks × 40 hours = 520 hours → $3.85/hour extra → regular rate $23.85. Overtime is then $35.78/hour, not $30. Ten OT hours a week costs $357.80, not $300 — a 19% underpayment if you compute on base rate alone. DOL audits routinely find exactly this error, and back-pay liability doubles with liquidated damages.

2026 threshold reality check: the DOL's 2024 rule raising the salary threshold to $58,656 was struck down by a federal court in late 2024, so the federal exemption threshold remains $684/week ($35,568/year) unless new rulemaking lands — verify current DOL guidance before classifying anyone. Practical effect: a $45K/year "manager" working 50-hour weeks is non-exempt under federal law and owed OT on all 10 extra hours. Misclassification is the costliest wage error there is: two years of back OT × 2x liquidated damages + attorneys' fees routinely turns a $15K underpayment into a $50K+ settlement.

Exempt vs Non-Exempt

TestRequirement
Salary basisFixed salary regardless of hours
Salary levelAbove DOL threshold
DutiesExecutive, admin, professional, or computer/creative

All three must pass. Job titles don't determine exemption - actual duties do. Misclassification is among the costliest wage violations.

The duties test is where job titles die. The executive exemption requires managing 2+ full-time employees AND a primary duty of management AND genuine authority over hiring and firing — a "shift lead" who can't hire or fire fails it regardless of salary. The administrative exemption requires independent judgment on significant matters, not just applying procedures. The professional exemption requires advanced specialized knowledge — accountants and engineers qualify; bookkeepers generally don't. Document the duties analysis per role in writing; "everyone's exempt" is an audit invitation.

The computer-employee exemption has its own pay test: $684/week salary OR $27.63/hour — the only exemption that allows hourly pay. A $30/hour systems analyst can be exempt; a $25/hour one cannot, no matter how technical the duties. State overlays bite harder: California demands roughly 2× minimum wage for exemption (about $68,640/year in 2026) — a worker exempt federally at $40K is non-exempt in California and owed daily overtime. Always apply the highest applicable threshold, federal or state.

Weighted Overtime (Multiple Rates)

Two jobs: 30 hrs at $18 + 20 hrs at $22 in one week (50 total).

  1. Total straight pay: 540 + 440 = $980
  2. Regular rate: 980 / 50 = $19.60
  3. OT premium: 10 hrs x (19.60 x 0.5) = $98
  4. Total: $1,078

Alternative legal method: OT at 1.5x the rate of the job performed during OT hours (if agreed in advance).

The fluctuating workweek method is the alternative most employers miss. A salaried non-exempt worker at $800/week who works 50 hours has a regular rate of $800 ÷ 50 = $16/hour, so the OT premium is only $8/hour × 10 = $80 (total $880) — versus $1,000 under straight time-and-a-half on the salary. Requirements are strict: clear mutual understanding that the salary covers all hours, hours genuinely fluctuate, and no state ban applies. The savings are real ($120/week here, $6,240/year) — but one missing requirement converts the whole arrangement into a violation.

Several states ban or restrict the fluctuating workweek outright — California and Pennsylvania among them require true 1.5× on the regular rate, killing the method entirely. Weighted-average OT has its own trap: when rates differ by shift, some states require OT at 1.5× the rate in effect during the overtime hours, not the blended rate. If the $22/hour shift ran the OT hours, that's $33/hour OT versus the $29.40 blended calculation. Check state law before choosing the cheaper method; the "savings" evaporate in penalties if you're wrong.

State Variations

  • California: Daily OT over 8 hrs (1.5x), double time over 12 hrs; 7th consecutive day rules.
  • Alaska, Nevada, others: Daily OT thresholds.
  • Colorado: OT on 12+ hour days or 12+ consecutive hours.
  • Comp time: Private employers generally cannot substitute comp time for OT pay.

When state and federal differ, the more generous to the employee applies.

The daily-OT map keeps growing beyond California. Alaska and Nevada trigger OT past 8 hours/day; Colorado past 12 hours/day (or 12 consecutive hours regardless of the workday boundary); Kentucky and others add 7th-day premiums. In 2026, several states index their exemption salary thresholds to inflation — Washington and New York now exceed $70K for larger employers. Multi-state payrolls need a state-by-state OT matrix; applying one national rule guarantees violations somewhere, and "we didn't know" isn't a defense.

Penalties scale fast enough to dwarf the original underpayment. California adds a 30-day waiting-time penalty (daily wages × 30 days) on unpaid OT — a $2,000 underpayment becomes $2,000 plus up to $6,000+ in penalties for a $25/hour worker, plus 10% interest. Private-sector comp time remains illegal federally: "take Friday off instead of OT pay" is a violation even if the employee prefers it (public employers may allow it under strict rules). When federal and state differ, the employee-favorable rule wins — document which rule you applied and why, every pay period.
