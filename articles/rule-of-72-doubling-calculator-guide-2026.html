The Rule and Why It Works

Years to Double ≈ 72 ÷ Annual Return %

At 8%: 72 ÷ 8 = 9 years to double. At 6%: 12 years. At 12%: 6 years. It works because ln(2) ≈ 0.693, and 72 has many divisors (2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12) making mental math clean.

Exact formula: n = ln(2) ÷ ln(1+r). At 8%, exact = 9.006 years — the rule is off by 2 days.

Run the rule on real returns, not nominal ones. At 8% nominal with 3% inflation, your real return is 5% — so 72 ÷ 5 = 14.4 years to double your purchasing power, not 9 years. Quoting the nominal 9-year doubling without inflation is how $1 million retirement "goals" quietly become $500,000 goals. Every doubling claim should carry its inflation assumption, or it's marketing.

Flip it for debt: an $8,000 credit-card balance at 24% APR doubles every 3 years (72 ÷ 24) — $8,000 → $16,000 → $32,000 in six years untouched. Minimum payments barely cover the interest, so the balance feels immortal because mathematically it nearly is. Tape the doubling time to the fridge: "3 years to $16K" motivates payoff far better than "24% APR."

Most accurate between 6-10%. For very low rates use Rule of 69.3; for high rates, Rule of 76 fits better.

For low rates, the Rule of 69.3 is sharper: at 3%, the Rule of 72 says 24 years but the exact answer is 23.45 — while 69.3 ÷ 3 = 23.1, noticeably closer. (69.3 is exact for continuous compounding, since ln(2) ≈ 0.693.) For everyday rates between 6% and 10%, though, 72's divisibility wins for mental math — 72 ÷ 6, 8, 9, and 12 all come out clean, which is exactly why bankers chose 72 over the more "correct" alternatives centuries ago.

The rule also prices decay: at a −7% annual decline, money halves every ~10.3 years (72 ÷ 7). Apply it to a shrinking business, a depreciating car (15%/year → value halves every 4.8 years), or a high-fee annuity quietly eroding principal. Any decay process can be quoted as a halving time — and halving times concentrate the mind wonderfully.

Everyday Uses

  • Retirement: $200K at 7% → doubles every ~10.3 years → ~$800K in 20 years, ~$1.6M in 30.
  • Inflation halving: At 3% inflation, purchasing power halves every 24 years (72 ÷ 3). $1M today buys like $500K for your kids.
  • Debt doubling: Credit card at 24% doubles the balance every 3 years if unpaid — the rule cuts both ways.
  • Salary: 4% annual raises double pay every 18 years.

Price your kid's college with it: education costs rising 5%/year double every 14.4 years — today's $30,000 sticker is $60,000 when your 4-year-old enrolls, and four years totals roughly $260,000. A 529 contribution plan built on today's sticker price will come up roughly half short. Work backward from the doubled number, not the current one — and revisit the math every few years, because 5% compounding punishes stale assumptions.

Apply it to healthcare and wages for a reality check: family premiums growing 6.5%/year double every 11 years — $12,000/year today becomes $24,000 in 11 years and $48,000 in 22. Salaries growing 3.5% double every ~20.6 years. When costs double in 11 years but pay doubles in 21, household budgets break on a predictable schedule — the rule makes the collision visible a decade early, while there's still time to raise savings rates or rethink coverage.

Tripling and Beyond

Years to Triple ≈ 114 ÷ Rate   |   Years to 10× ≈ 230 ÷ Rate

At 8%: triple in ~14.3 years, 10× in ~28.8 years. These come from ln(3) ≈ 1.099 and ln(10) ≈ 2.303 scaled like the 72. Handy for sanity-checking long-range projections without a calculator.

Memorize the quadrupling shortcut: 144 ÷ rate. At 8%, money quadruples in 18 years — check: $10,000 × 1.0818 = $39,960. Two doublings back-to-back, one division. Useful for generational planning: grandparents funding a newborn's account can quote the 18-year quadrupling directly against college timelines, and a 6% return quadruples money every 24 years — almost exactly one generation.

Sanity-check 10-bagger dreams with the same trick: 230 ÷ rate gives years to 10×. At 7%, that's 32.9 years versus an exact 33.1 — off by two months. Now invert it for pitches: a stock promising 10× in 10 years demands 23%/year compounded — a rate essentially no diversified portfolio has ever sustained for a full decade. When anyone promises 10×, divide 230 by their timeline and ask whether the required rate has ever been achieved by anything you'd actually hold. If the answer is no, the pitch is a lottery ticket wearing a spreadsheet.
