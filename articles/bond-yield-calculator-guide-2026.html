Current Yield vs Yield to Maturity

Current Yield = Annual Coupon ÷ Current Price

A $1,000 bond with 5% coupon ($50/year) trading at $950: current yield = 5.26%. But current yield ignores the $50 gain at maturity.

YTM ≈ [Coupon + (Face − Price) ÷ Years] ÷ [(Face + Price) ÷ 2]

Same bond, 10 years left: YTM ≈ [$50 + $5] ÷ $975 = 5.64% — the true annualized return if held to maturity.

Yield-to-call matters more than YTM on premium bonds. Take a 5% coupon, $1,000 face bond callable at $1,020 in 3 years, priced at $1,050. Yield-to-call ≈ [$50 + ($1,020 − $1,050) ÷ 3] ÷ $1,035 = $40 ÷ $1,035 = 3.87% — far below its yield to maturity. When rates fall, issuers call and refinance; your upside is capped at the call price while your downside isn't. On any bond trading above its call price, quote YTC first and treat YTM as fiction.

Compute the tax-equivalent yield before comparing munis to taxable bonds: 4% ÷ (1 − tax rate). At a 32% bracket, a 4% muni equals 5.88% taxable; at 12%, it equals just 4.55% — munis only win in high brackets. And never buy municipal bonds inside an IRA or 401(k): you accept the muni's lower yield in exchange for a tax break you don't need in a tax-sheltered account. That's paying for insurance on a house you don't own.

The Price-Yield Seesaw

Bond prices and yields move inversely. When market rates rise, existing bonds (with lower coupons) fall in price until their yield matches.

Duration measures sensitivity: a 10-year bond with duration 8 loses ~8% per 1% rate rise. Longer maturity = bigger swings.

Respect duration risk with 2022's lesson: long-term Treasuries fell roughly 30% as rates rose — a duration of 15 means a 2% rate increase costs about 30% of principal. In 2026, with the rate path still uncertain, match duration to your horizon ruthlessly: money needed in 2 years has no business in a 10-year bond fund, no matter how tempting the yield looks. Duration isn't a footnote; it's the risk number.

Use the TIPS breakeven as the market's inflation forecast. If 10-year nominal Treasuries yield 4.5% and 10-year TIPS yield 2.0%, breakeven inflation = 2.5%. Expect inflation above 2.5% over the decade? Buy TIPS. Below? Buy nominals. You're not guessing about inflation in the abstract — you're trading directly against the market's priced-in expectation, which is a much fairer fight.

Premium vs Discount Bonds

  • Discount bond (price < face): YTM > coupon rate. You earn the coupon plus price appreciation.
  • Premium bond (price > face): YTM < coupon rate. Part of each coupon repays the premium.
  • Tax twist: Premium amortization is deductible against interest income; discount accretion (OID) is taxed annually even though you receive nothing until maturity.

2026 context: Bonds bought in 2020-2021 at low coupons trade at deep discounts — their YTMs now look attractive even though prices fell.

OID phantom income is the discount bond's nasty surprise. Buy at $850 with a $1,000 face and 10 years left: the $150 of accretion is taxed as ordinary income at roughly $15/year — yet you receive $0 in cash until maturity. You're paying real tax on imaginary income every April. In taxable accounts this stings; inside an IRA the phantom income is irrelevant. Place discount bonds by account type, not just by yield.

The premium side has its own election: premium amortization. Buy the $1,170 bond (from the section 1 table) and the $170 premium amortizes at roughly $17/year, deductible against your interest income — shrinking the taxable portion of each coupon. But the election isn't automatic for every bond type, and once made it applies going forward. Ask your broker whether amortization is being applied; unclaimed amortization is a deduction you're donating to the Treasury.

Building a Bond Ladder

A ladder staggers maturities (e.g., 1-5 years) so something always matures:

  1. Buy equal amounts of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5-year bonds/CDs.
  2. When the 1-year matures, reinvest into a new 5-year.
  3. Result: annual liquidity + blended yield + automatic rate adaptation.

$100,000 across 5 rungs at ~4.5% average yields $4,500/year with a bond maturing every 12 months — ideal for near-term goals and emergency reserves.

Consider the barbell as an alternative to the classic ladder: instead of 1-through-5-year rungs, hold 1-year T-bills plus 10-year bonds for a similar average duration. Higher convexity means the barbell outperforms the ladder when rates move sharply in either direction. The ladder wins on simplicity and predictable annual cash flow; the barbell wins on rate-move optionality. Match the structure to your temperament, not just the yield curve.

Run the 2026 ladder autopilot: $100,000 across 1-5 year rungs averaging ~4.3% generates $4,300/year, with a rung maturing every 12 months. When it matures, reinvest into a new 5-year rung at the prevailing rate — if rates rise to 5.5%, next year's income steps up automatically with zero decisions required. That self-adjusting feature is the ladder's real product; the yield is just the packaging.
