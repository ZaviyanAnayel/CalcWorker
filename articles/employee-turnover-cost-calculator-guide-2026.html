The True Cost Formula

Turnover Cost = 50-200% of Annual Salary (by role complexity)

Senior/specialized150-200%$90K-$120K
Executive200%+$200K+

Industry benchmarks sharpen the 50-200% range considerably. Retail and hospitality turnover runs $3,500-$5,000 per frontline exit (high volume, lower complexity), while a single software-engineer departure costs $100K-$150K+ in recruiting fees, ramp time, and lost velocity. In 2026's tight skilled-labor market, specialized roles skew toward the top of the band: a $140K data scientist at 150% costs $210K all-in. Scale it: 15 departures in a 100-person org at a $70K average salary means roughly $880K-$1.3M walking out the door every year.

The salary multiple understates revenue damage for customer-facing roles. An account executive holding a $1.2M book who quits typically puts 10-20% of that revenue at risk during the 3-6 month transition — $120K-$240K of churn exposure stacked on top of the $90K replacement cost. Then comes the morale tax: teams with a recent departure show 5-10% productivity dips for a quarter as survivors absorb the load and quietly start interviewing. Contagion is real — one visible quit often triggers two more within 90 days.

Cost Breakdown: $75K Employee

  • Separation: $3,000 (exit admin, severance, unemployment)
  • Vacancy: $15,000 (lost productivity, overtime for others, 2 months)
  • Recruiting: $12,000 (ads, recruiter fees, interview time)
  • Onboarding: $8,000 (training, ramp-up)
  • Ramp productivity loss: $25,000 (6 months to full productivity)
  • Total: ~$63,000 (84% of salary)

The $63K total misses management overhead that timesheets never capture. A hiring manager spending 25 hours across intake, resume review, interviews, and debriefs at a $65/hour loaded rate adds $1,625; five panel interviewers at 3 hours each × $60/hour adds $900; HR coordination and scheduling adds another $500. Temp or contractor coverage for the 8-10 week gap at $45/hour for 20 hours/week adds $7,200-$9,000. The realistic all-in for the $75K role: $72K-$80K — essentially 100% of salary, not 84%.

Customer and error costs are the uncounted remainder. New hires in support or operations run 2-3x the error rate for their first 90 days — in a $75K ops role processing $2M of monthly transactions, a 0.5% error uptick costs $10K/month, or $30K over the ramp quarter. Client-facing roles add relationship risk: enterprise clients consistently rate "my rep changed" as a top-3 churn driver. Budget 10-15% of the role's managed revenue as at-risk during transition and the retention business case writes itself.

Retention ROI

Team of 50, 20% turnover, avg salary $70K: 10 departures x $59K avg cost = $590K/year in turnover.

Cut turnover to 10%: Save $295K/year.

Retention investments that pay:

  • $5K raise for 50 people = $250K - pays for itself if it prevents 5 departures
  • Manager training: bad managers drive 70% of voluntary quits
  • Flexible work: costs little, retains enormously
  • Career paths: #1 reason people leave is "no growth"

Targeted stay bonuses beat across-the-board raises on ROI. A $3K-$5K retention bonus for 20 flight-risk top performers costs $60K-$100K versus $250K for a blanket $5K raise — and it prevents the specific departures that hurt most. Structure it with a 12-month clawback so it retains rather than rewards past loyalty. The math: preventing 5 departures at $59K each saves $295K against a $100K program — a 3:1 return. Even a 50% success rate (2-3 saves) breaks even, which makes this the safest retention bet in the budget.

Manager training carries the best-documented ROI in retention: Gallup attributes 70% of engagement variance to the direct manager, so replacing a bad manager's 30% team churn with a 15% average on a 50-person org saves ~7 departures × $59K = $413K/year. A $25K manager development program that moves the needle even 20% pays for itself three times over. The cheapest lever of all: stay interviews — a 30-minute quarterly conversation costing ~$40 of manager time surfaces flight risks a full 6 months before the resignation letter.

Measuring and Reducing Turnover

  1. Track voluntary vs involuntary separately - only voluntary is fixable by retention.
  2. Exit interviews: Ask why; fix patterns, not anecdotes.
  3. Stay interviews: Ask current stars what keeps them - cheaper than exits.
  4. First 90 days: 20% of turnover happens here - fix onboarding first.
  5. eNPS: "Would you recommend working here?" predicts quits 6 months out.

Compute turnover correctly or the number lies: Turnover Rate = (Voluntary Departures ÷ Average Headcount) × 100, annualized. Ten quits in a 50-person org over 6 months isn't "10 quits" — it's 20% for the half, 40% annualized, a crisis the raw count hides. 2026 benchmarks: tech ~13%, professional services ~15%, healthcare ~20%, retail/hospitality 50-60%+. Track regretted versus non-regretted separately: losing 5% of top performers hurts more than losing 15% of low performers, and only the regretted number belongs in retention ROI math.

eNPS turns sentiment into an early-warning number: score = % promoters (9-10) minus % detractors (0-6) on "would you recommend working here?" Scores below +10 predict elevated quits within two quarters; above +40 is best-in-class. Run it quarterly with 2-3 driver questions — teams scoring under 0 need intervention now, not at review time. Pair it with 30/60/90-day new-hire pulse surveys: 20% of turnover lands in the first 90 days, and structured onboarding check-ins cut early attrition 25-40%.
