The effective-limit advantage favors Roth for max-funders. At a 24% tax rate, a $7,500 Roth contribution equals $9,868 of pre-tax money ($7,500 ÷ 0.76) — because the Roth contribution is made with after-tax dollars, it shelters more economic value than the same nominal traditional contribution. If you can afford to max out either way, Roth quietly gives high-bracket savers extra tax-advantaged space the traditional side can't match.

Learn the two 5-year rules before you need them. Rule one: the contribution clock starts January 1 of the year you first open ANY Roth IRA — withdraw earnings before five years plus age 59½, and you owe tax plus a 10% penalty. Rule two: each Roth conversion starts its own 5-year clock for the 10% early-withdrawal penalty on converted principal if you're under 59½. Open a Roth at 30 with even $100 to start the first clock ticking decades before you'll need it.

30-Year Math: $7,500/Year at 8%

Scenario: 30-year-old, 24% bracket now, expects 22% in retirement.

  • Roth: Contribute $7,500 after-tax yearly → grows to $850,000, all tax-free.
  • Traditional: Contribute $7,500 pre-tax yearly → grows to $850,000, minus 22% tax = $663,000 spendable. But you saved $1,800/year in taxes upfront — invested, that is worth ~$204,000.
  • True comparison: Roth $850,000 vs Traditional $663,000 + $204,000 = $867,000 — nearly a tie, with Traditional slightly ahead if you invest the tax savings (most people do not).

The tiebreaker rule: identical tax rates now vs later = identical results. Choose based on which rate will be higher.

Meet the RMD tax torpedo that Roth dollars dodge entirely. A $1.2 million traditional IRA at 75 forces a first RMD of $1,200,000 ÷ 24.6 = $48,780. Stack $36,000 of Social Security on top and a single filer lands near $85,000 of income — well into the 24% bracket with higher Medicare premiums attached. Every dollar converted to Roth in your 60s is a dollar that never appears in that RMD calculation.

A concrete conversion play: at 62, retired, with $50,000 of income, convert $40,000 to fill the 12% bracket — tax cost $4,800. That $40,000 then compounds tax-free forever. Left in traditional and withdrawn later at 24%, the tax would be $9,600. One year's conversion saves $4,800; repeated annually through your 60s, the lifetime savings reach deep into five figures — all for moving money between your own accounts.

2026 Income Limits

  • Roth IRA: Full contribution under $153K single / $242K joint MAGI; phases out across $153K–$168K single / $242K–$252K joint. High earners use the backdoor Roth (nondeductible traditional contribution → immediate conversion).
  • Traditional deductibility: If covered by a workplace plan, deduction phases out at $81K-$91K single / $129K-$149K joint. Without a workplace plan, fully deductible at any income.
  • Pro-rata rule: Backdoor Roths get taxed proportionally if you hold other pre-tax IRA balances — roll those into a 401(k) first.

2026 figures (IRS Notice 2025-67): the IRA contribution limit rose to $7,500 ($8,600 at 50+ with the newly indexed $1,100 catch-up). Roth IRA phaseouts: single $153,000-$168,000, joint $242,000-$252,000. Traditional IRA deductibility when covered by a workplace plan: single $81,000-$91,000, joint $129,000-$149,000. Income limits creep upward most years — recheck eligibility every January rather than assuming last year's answer still holds.

The pro-rata trap ruins backdoor Roths with real numbers. You hold an $85,000 pre-tax IRA and add a $7,500 nondeductible contribution, then convert $7,500. Taxable portion = $7,500 × ($85,000 ÷ $92,500) = $6,892 — the conversion is 92% taxable. The fix: roll the $85,000 pre-tax balance into your 401(k) first, isolating the after-tax dollars. Then the same conversion is roughly $0 in tax. Sequence matters more than paperwork.

Strategic Plays

  1. Young/low earner: Roth — pay low taxes now, enjoy tax-free growth for decades.
  2. Peak earner: Traditional — take the deduction at 32-37%, convert in low-income years.
  3. Roth conversions: In gap years (early retirement before RMDs/Social Security), convert traditional balances at low brackets.
  4. Estate planning: Roth IRAs pass to heirs tax-free (10-year rule still applies); traditional IRAs hand heirs a tax bill.

The spousal IRA is the most overlooked $1 million decision in retirement planning. A non-working spouse can contribute $7,500/year based on the working spouse's income — $15,000 per household. That extra $7,500/year at 8% for 30 years = $849,600 of additional tax-advantaged wealth. Couples who fund only the earner's IRA are voluntarily leaving nearly seven figures on the table over a career.

Start Roths for your kids: a teen with $7,500 of summer earnings can hold $7,500 in a custodial Roth IRA — at 8% for 50 years, that's $351,700 tax-free. And think about heirs: an inherited traditional IRA forces taxable withdrawals under the 10-year rule, while an inherited Roth IRA follows the same 10-year schedule with $0 in tax. Choosing Roth in your peak years is partly an estate-planning gift to your children.
