The BMI Formula

BMI = Weight (kg) ÷ Height (m)²   |   BMI = 703 × Weight (lb) ÷ Height (in)²

Example: 5'9" (69 in), 180 lb: 703 x 180 / 69^2 = 26.6 (overweight category).

Try a metric example to lock in the method: 82 kg at 1.75 m. Square the height first (1.75 x 1.75 = 3.0625), then divide: 82 / 3.0625 = 26.8. The most common error is skipping the square: 82 / 1.75 = 46.9, which looks absurd and sends people panicking into the wrong category. A second trap is entering height in centimeters (175) instead of meters, producing a BMI near zero. Always convert: 175 cm = 1.75 m, 82 kg stays as-is.

For children aged 2-19, raw BMI is meaningless without the growth chart: pediatricians use BMI-for-age percentiles, with overweight at the 85th percentile and obesity at the 95th. A 10-year-old boy at the 90th percentile needs attention even though his BMI of 19 looks "healthy" on the adult chart. Pregnancy flips the script too: pre-pregnancy BMI sets the weight-gain target (25-35 lb for normal BMI), and the number is frozen as a reference rather than tracked. For adults over 65, the curve flattens: BMIs of 23-28 associate with the lowest mortality, and chasing 22 can cost needed muscle reserve.

What BMI Gets Wrong

  • Athletes: A muscular 5'10", 210 lb lifter scores BMI 30.1 ("obese") at 12% body fat.
  • Older adults: Muscle loss with age means BMI can look "healthy" while body fat is high ("skinny fat").
  • Ethnicity: Asian populations face metabolic risk at lower BMIs (WHO suggests 23+ as increased risk); standard cutoffs were built on European data.
  • Distribution: BMI ignores where fat sits - visceral belly fat drives disease far more than hip/thigh fat.

Height extremes distort the formula because it squares height instead of cubing it. Researcher Nick Trefethen proposed a corrected version: 1.3 x kg / height(m)^2.5. Take a 6'5" (1.956 m), 230 lb (104.3 kg) man: standard BMI = 104.3 / 3.825 = 27.3 ("overweight"), corrected = 135.6 / 5.345 = 25.4 (upper healthy). Short adults get the opposite bias. Fluid retention is another blind spot: heart-failure patients can swing 5-10 lb of water, moving BMI 1.5+ points with zero fat change.

The "metabolically healthy obese" nuance matters: 10-30% of people with obesity have normal blood pressure, lipids, and glucose today — but roughly half develop metabolic problems within 10 years, so "healthy" is usually a phase, not a category. Sex differences add another wrinkle: at BMI 25, women average about 10% more body fat than men, which is why some researchers argue women's cutoffs should sit roughly 2 BMI points lower.

Better Metrics to Pair With BMI

The 0.5 rule: Keep your waist less than half your height. A 5'9" (69") person should target a waist under 34.5 inches - simple, and research shows it predicts risk better than BMI alone.

Read the table left to right: BMI alone misranks all four. The pairing protocol is simple — BMI 27 with a 33" waist at 5'10" is likely fine; BMI 27 with a 41" waist is not. Compute waist-to-height as the tiebreaker: 41 / 70 = 0.586, well over the 0.5 line. When BMI and waist disagree, trust the waist; when they agree, you have your answer twice over.

Using BMI Sensibly

  1. Calculate BMI as a screening starting point, not a diagnosis.
  2. Add waist measurement - if both are elevated, act.
  3. Track trends over months, not single readings.
  4. Athletes: skip BMI; use body fat % and waist-to-height.
  5. Discuss with your doctor before making major changes based on BMI alone.

Convert a BMI into a concrete weight target with one formula: target weight = target BMI x height(in)^2 / 703. Our 5'9" (69"), 180-lb example at BMI 26.6: aiming for 24.9 gives 24.9 x 4761 / 703 = 168.6 lb — an 11.4-lb loss, roughly 12 weeks at 1 lb/week. That beats vague "lose some weight" because it sets the finish line, the pace, and the calorie math (500 cal/day deficit) in one move.

Know where BMI actually bites: many insurers add premium loading above BMI 30 (some require medical exams above 35), and military enlistment has hard BMI caps with tape-test waivers. Recheck quarterly, not weekly — BMI moves slowly, and monthly weighing with quarterly BMI computation filters the noise. A single high reading after holidays is data; three rising quarters is a trend.
