The Cash Flow Formula

Monthly Cash Flow = Rent − (PITI + Vacancy + Maintenance + CapEx + Management + Utilities)

Beginners count PITI only and call the rest "profit." Professionals budget every line:

  • Vacancy: 5-8% of rent
  • Maintenance: 5-10% (older homes higher)
  • CapEx reserves: 5-10% (roof, HVAC, appliances)
  • Management: 8-10% (even self-managers should budget it)

Itemize capex instead of guessing a percentage. Roof $12,000 ÷ 25 years = $40/month. HVAC $7,500 ÷ 15 = $42/month. Water heater $1,600 ÷ 10 = $13/month. Appliances $4,000 ÷ 12 = $28/month. That's $123/month in reserves before a single repair call — on a $2,100 rental, nearly 6% of gross spoken for by predictable replacements. Skip the reserve and the year-8 HVAC bill wipes out two full years of "profit" in one invoice.

The landlord-paid utilities trap: water, sewer, and trash at $140/month on a $2,100 rental silently consumes 6.7% of gross revenue. Submeter where legal or bill tenants back — utility-included rents need to be priced $150+/month higher just to break even, and tenants with free water use dramatically more of it. Every utility you pay is a cost you can't control and a tenant can't conserve.

Negative $298/month. This "deal" loses money on a fully-loaded basis — exactly why the 50% rule exists: assume half the rent vanishes to expenses before debt service. $2,100 × 50% = $1,050 vs $1,490 P&I → fails the rule instantly.

Rate sensitivity decides more deals than purchase price in 2026. The same $280,000 property: at 5.5% the P&I is $1,272/month and cash flow is roughly −$80/month; at 8% the P&I is $1,650/month and cash flow craters to about −$458/month. A 2.5-point rate swing moves monthly cash flow $378 — more than most investors negotiate off the price. When rates fall, rerun every rejected deal; the math changes faster than the listings.

The 15-year loan twist: $224,000 at 6% over 15 years = $1,890/month P&I, pushing cash flow to about −$698/month — yet you're building $900+/month in principal paydown from day one. Negative cash flow plus massive forced equity is a valid strategy if your W-2 income can feed the property monthly. It's a savings plan disguised as a bad investment; just never confuse the two on paper.

Rules of Thumb

  • 1% rule: Monthly rent ≥ 1% of purchase price ($280K → $2,800 rent needed; actual $2,100 → fail).
  • 50% rule: Expenses ≈ 50% of gross rent over time.
  • Cash-on-cash: Annual cash flow ÷ cash invested. Target 8-12%+.

These are screening tools, not underwriting. Markets where nothing passes the 1% rule (most coastal cities in 2026) require appreciation plays, not cash-flow plays — be honest about which game you are playing.

Add the $100-per-door rule to your screening stack: each unit should clear $100/month after all expenses — a duplex needs $200, a fourplex $400. The walkthrough property at −$298/month fails by nearly $500 against this bar. It's crude, but crude filters save hours: any listing failing $100/door needs heroic assumptions (or a much bigger down payment) to become a deal.

Know where each rule lives. The 1% rule still screens deals in parts of the Midwest and South in 2026 — a $120,000 home renting for $1,200 passes cleanly. On the coasts it's fiction, and screening coastal listings with it rejects everything worth owning. Learn your market's prevailing rent-to-price ratio first, then pick the rule that fits the terrain — applying the wrong rule either rejects every deal or blesses dogs.

Making the Numbers Work

  1. Bigger down payment cuts P&I but also cuts cash-on-cash — model both.
  2. Value-add: Forced appreciation via renovation can lift rents 15-25%.
  3. House hacking: Live in one unit of a duplex; your "rent" offsets the mortgage.
  4. Lower rate: Every 1% rate drop on $224K saves ~$150/month.
  5. Walk away: The best deal analyzer skill is saying no to 95% of listings.

Run BRRRR numbers concretely: buy $150,000 + $40,000 rehab = $190,000 all-in; appraised ARV $240,000; rent $1,900. Refinance at 80% LTV = $192,000 — virtually every dollar comes back out, ~$0 left in the deal. Even $150/month of post-refi cash flow is an infinite cash-on-cash return. The refinance is the business model; the rent is the bonus. BRRRR fails only when the ARV appraisal disappoints — so comp the ARV like your capital depends on it, because it does.

Hunt rate arbitrage instead of just price cuts. Assumable FHA/VA loans at 3-4% still surface on some listings — assuming a $220,000 balance at 3.5% versus borrowing new at 7% saves roughly $460/month in P&I. Seller financing at 5-6% with 10% down often beats bank terms on speed and flexibility. In a 7% market, creative finance manufactures more cash flow than a $20,000 price reduction ever will.
