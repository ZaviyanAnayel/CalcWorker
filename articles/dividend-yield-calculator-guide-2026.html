Dividend Yield Formula

Dividend Yield = Annual Dividends per Share ÷ Current Share Price

A stock paying $2.40/year trading at $60 yields 4.0%. If the price drops to $48 with the dividend unchanged, yield rises to 5.0% — but investigate why the price fell before celebrating.

Yield traps: Double-digit yields usually signal a dividend about to be cut. Check the payout ratio (dividends ÷ earnings): under 60% is comfortable; over 90% is fragile.

Refine the payout ratio with free cash flow, not earnings. Earnings can be massaged with depreciation assumptions and one-time adjustments; cash is harder to fake. A utility paying $2.40 on $3.00 of free cash flow per share runs an 80% FCF payout — full but sustainable for a regulated monopoly with predictable demand. The identical 80% at a cyclical manufacturer flashing "cheap" is a dividend cut waiting for a recession. Always compare payout ratios within sectors, never across them.

Do the cut math before chasing yield. An 8% yielder slashes its dividend 40%: your yield-on-cost drops to 4.8% and the stock typically falls 15-25% on the announcement. A $50,000 position generating $4,000/year becomes $2,400 of income on ~$40,000 of principal — a double wound. This is why double-digit yields deserve suspicion, not celebration: the market is usually pricing the cut before it happens. Diversify dividend income across 15+ payers so no single cut exceeds 7% of your income.

Dividend Aristocrats and Growth

Dividend Aristocrats (25+ years of consecutive increases) averaged strong long-term returns with lower volatility. But yield alone misleads:

StockPriceDividendYieldPayout Ratio
Example A (utility)$60$2.404.0%65%
Example B (tech)$180$1.200.67%15%

Stock B's low yield reflects a rising price, not stinginess — its dividend grew 12%/year for a decade. Dividend growth rate often matters more than starting yield.

Dividend growth beats starting yield — run the crossover. Stock A: 2% yield growing 12%/year. Stock B: 6% yield growing 2%/year. Yield-on-cost after 12 years: A = 2% × 1.1212 = 7.8%; B = 6% × 1.0212 = 7.6%. The "low yield" stock pays you MORE per original dollar by year 12, and its total return (price appreciation from that growth) usually wins by far more. Income investors who screen on yield alone systematically buy the B's and miss the A's.

Know the Aristocrat admission rules: S&P 500 membership plus 25 consecutive annual dividend increases — not just payments. Only 60-70 companies qualify at any time, the index rebalances annually, and cutters are ejected. "Dividend Kings" (50+ years) number around 50. Never buy from memory: check the current constituent list, because last decade's Aristocrat may have been demoted after freezing its payout.

DRIP: The Compounding Engine

Dividend Reinvestment Plans buy more shares automatically, commission-free:

$10,000 at 4% yield, 6% price growth, 20 years:

  • Taking dividends as cash: ~$32,071
  • Reinvesting (DRIP): ~$38,500 — 20% more from compounding alone

Over 30 years the DRIP advantage widens further. Reinvest while accumulating; switch to cash payouts in retirement.

Tax drag quietly taxes your compounding even when you reinvest. $100,000 at a 4% yield = $4,000 in dividends; at the 15% qualified rate that's $600/year owed even though you never touched the cash — over 20 years, roughly $20,000+ of compounded leakage in a taxable account. Asset location solves it: hold high-yield payers inside IRAs/401(k)s where the drag is zero, and keep taxable accounts for qualified-growth stocks with low current yield.

Basis bookkeeping is DRIP's hidden chore: 20 years of quarterly reinvestments creates 80 separate tax lots to track at sale. Brokers track "covered" shares (post-2011) automatically, but older DRIP positions need your own records — lose them and you'll overpay. One genuine perk: some companies offer 3-5% DRIP discounts on direct-stock-plan purchases. A 5% discount on every reinvested dividend is free money if you'd hold the stock anyway.

Dividend Tax Rates 2026

  • Qualified dividends: 0%, 15%, or 20% based on taxable income (most US stocks held 60+ days qualify)
  • Ordinary dividends (REITs, MLPs, short holds): taxed as income
  • Asset location: Hold REITs/high-yield in IRAs; hold qualified-dividend stocks in taxable accounts
  • Foreign dividends may face withholding tax — partially recoverable via the foreign tax credit

2026 brackets for qualified dividends: 0% up to $49,450 single / $98,900 joint of taxable income; 15% up to $545,500 / $613,700; 20% above. Then add the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax above $200,000 single / $250,000 joint (thresholds not inflation-indexed) — a high earner's "15% qualified" dividends actually face 18.8%, and top-bracket payers face 23.8%. Quote your all-in marginal rate when comparing dividend stocks to bonds, not just the base bracket.

Mind the 61-day holding rule: hold the stock more than 60 days during the 121-day window around the ex-dividend date, or the dividend is reclassified as ordinary income. Buy the day before ex-div and flip right after, and your "qualified" 15% becomes 24-37%. Foreign dividends add another layer: treaty countries typically withhold 15% at source — a $1,000 Swiss dividend arrives as $850, with the $150 partially recoverable via the foreign tax credit on Form 1116. Non-treaty countries can withhold 25-35%.
