SEP IRA Contribution Formula

Employers (including self-employed individuals) can contribute up to 25% of compensation, capped at $72,000 for 2026. For self-employed filers, the effective rate is ~20% of net profit after the SE tax deduction.

Max SEP = min(25% × Compensation, $72,000)
Self-Employed Effective Rate ≈ 20% of (Net Profit − ½ SE Tax)

Example: $150,000 net self-employment profit → effective contribution base ~$139,000 → max SEP ≈ $27,800.

The SEP IRA ceiling for 2026 is $72,000 (25% of compensation) — but the S-corp trap catches owners off guard: the 25% applies to W-2 wages only, not K-1 distributions. Pay yourself an $80,000 salary with $200,000 in distributions and your max is 25% × $80,000 = $20,000 — not $70,000. Aggressive low-salary strategies that save SE tax simultaneously shrink your SEP room; model both effects before setting salary.

The SEP's superpower is its retroactive deadline: you can establish AND fund a SEP by the extended filing deadline — October 15, 2027 for tax year 2026. Staring at an $18,000 tax bill this April with an extension filed? Open a SEP in September, contribute retroactively for 2026, and cut the bill. No other retirement plan lets you create the deduction this far after year-end — it's the procrastinator's time machine.

At $100,000 profit: SEP allows ~$18,600; Solo 401(k) allows $24,500 + ~$18,600 = $43,100. Solo 401(k) wins for most solo operators — SEP wins only for simplicity or when you have employees (same % required for all).

Run the real numbers at $60,000 of profit: net earnings after the SE-tax deduction ≈ $55,761. SEP IRA allows 20% ≈ $11,152. A Solo 401(k) allows the $24,500 employee deferral PLUS the same $11,152 employer piece = $35,652 — a $24,500 wider shelter. At a 24% marginal rate, that's $5,880 of extra tax saved in year one alone, every year. Below ~$60K of profit, the Solo 401(k)'s employee deferral dominates so completely the comparison isn't close.

When SEP still wins: you have employees and want near-zero administration — one Form 5305-SEP, no annual Form 5500 filing, no plan documents to maintain. And don't forget the SIMPLE IRA alternative for 2026: $17,000 in employee deferrals plus a 3% employer match. Under roughly $40,000 of profit, SIMPLE's structure often shelters more than a SEP with far less math.

Deadlines and Setup

  • Setup deadline: Tax filing deadline including extensions (unlike Solo 401(k), which must be established by year-end).
  • Contribution deadline: Same — file by October 15 with extension and contribute retroactively for 2026.
  • Employees: Must contribute the same percentage for all eligible employees (21+, 3 of last 5 years, $750+ compensation) — expensive with staff.
  • No loans: Unlike 401(k)s, SEP IRAs do not allow loans.

The controlled-group trap is the SEP's hidden landmine. Own two businesses? Employees of both may require coverage. A side LLC with one part-time assistant can force SEP contributions for that assistant — and the same-percentage rule is merciless: contribute 15% for yourself and you owe 15% for every eligible employee. Three employees earning $50,000 each turns your $20,000 personal contribution into a $42,500 total outlay. Map all your entities before choosing SEP.

Calendar discipline matters because the SEP has no catch-up contributions at 50+ — another Solo 401(k) edge worth $8,000/year. And the deadline is a cliff, not a slope: miss October 15 (with extension) and the 2026 deduction is lost forever; unlike 401(k) deferrals, there's no corrective mechanism. Set two reminders — one to establish, one to fund — because the IRS grants no grace period.

Who Should Use a SEP IRA

  1. High-income freelancers wanting big deductions with minimal admin.
  2. Side-gig earners with W-2 401(k)s — SEP on freelance income stacks on top (watch the $70K combined limit per employer... separate employers means separate limits).
  3. Avoid if: you have employees (costly), want Roth, or need catch-up contributions — use Solo 401(k) instead.

W-2 plus side-gig stacking is where SEPs quietly shine. Earn $140,000 salary (401(k) maxed at the day job) plus $50,000 freelance: the SEP on freelance income ≈ 20% × $46,464 ≈ $9,293 — and because it's a separate employer, it gets its own $72,000 cap. Total sheltered: $24,500 + employer match + $9,293. The day job's 401(k) doesn't reduce SEP room by a dollar; only the $72,000-per-employer ceiling applies.

Real-world fit: a real-estate agent with $120,000 in commission income and no employees. The Solo 401(k) shelters more on paper — but the SEP takes five minutes to open with no annual filings. A funded SEP beats an unfunded perfect plan every time. If complexity is the reason you saved $0 last year, choose the SEP, fund it by April, and optimize next year.
