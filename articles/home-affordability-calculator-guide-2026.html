The 28/36 Rule Explained

Lenders use two debt-to-income (DTI) ratios. The front-end ratio (housing costs ÷ gross income) should stay under 28%. The back-end ratio (all monthly debts ÷ gross income) should stay under 36% for the best rates, though many programs allow up to 43-50%.

Max Housing Payment = Gross Monthly Income × 0.28
Max Total Debt Payments = Gross Monthly Income × 0.36

On a $120,000 salary ($10,000/month): max housing = $2,800/month; max all debts = $3,600/month. If you already pay $700/month in car and student loans, your housing budget shrinks to $2,900 — the back-end ratio becomes the binding constraint.

Program overlays change the ratios dramatically for the same borrower. FHA allows 31% front-end and up to 43% back-end - stretchable to 56.9% with automated underwriting approval - so our $120,000 earner could carry $4,300/month in housing under FHA versus $2,800 under the classic 28% rule. VA flips the script entirely: no front-end ratio at all, with a 41% back-end guideline softened by residual income. The "28/36 rule" is really the conventional prime-borrower rule; your actual ceiling depends on which loan program you use, which is why pre-approval shopping across programs matters.

The gross-versus-net gap is where affordability breaks down emotionally. That $10,000/month gross becomes roughly $7,200-$7,600 take-home after federal, state, and FICA taxes in most states. A $2,800 housing payment is 28% of gross but 37-39% of what actually hits your account - before retirement contributions, health premiums, or childcare. Lenders underwrite on gross; you live on net. Smart buyers translate the approved amount into a take-home percentage and flinch if it crosses 40%.

$2,123/month at 6.75% supports a loan of about $327,300. With 10% down, the max price is roughly $363,700. Notice how taxes and insurance consume ~25% of the budget — buyers who ignore them overestimate by $50,000+.

High-tax states rewrite this example. In New Jersey, with effective property taxes near 2.4%, a $363,700 home carries about $727/month in taxes instead of $417 - an extra $310/month that must come from the $2,800 budget. Working backward, the supportable loan shrinks to roughly $280,000 and the max price to about $311,000: a $52,000 haircut purely from the tax line. In low-tax Colorado at 0.55%, the same budget stretches past $400,000. Never use a national affordability estimate without plugging in your county's actual effective tax rate.

HOA dues deserve the same reverse-engineering treatment. A $400/month HOA - common in condos and planned communities - consumes the same budget space as roughly $60,000 in purchase price at 6.75% rates. Worse, HOAs rise: a $400 fee growing 5% annually becomes $652 in ten years, while your mortgage payment stays fixed. When comparing a $360,000 house with no HOA against a $340,000 condo with a $400 HOA, the house is meaningfully cheaper despite the higher sticker price. Always add HOA to the "taxes and insurance" subtraction step.

Hidden Costs That Break Budgets

  • Maintenance: Budget 1-2% of home value yearly ($3,600-$7,200 on a $360K home). Newer homes trend lower; older homes higher.
  • HOA: $200-$500/month is common and counts in your DTI.
  • Utilities jump: Moving from apartment to house often adds $150-$300/month.
  • Closing costs: 2-5% of price due at signing — $7,000-$18,000 on our example.
  • Rate reality: Every 1% rate increase cuts buying power ~10%. At 7.75% instead of 6.75%, our buyer's max price falls to ~$330,000.

The first-year cash requirement extends well beyond down payment and closing costs, and buyers consistently under-budget it. On our $363,700 example: closing costs of $11,000-$18,000, moving costs of $3,000-$5,000, immediate needs like locks, window coverings, and minor repairs of $4,000-$8,000, plus utility deposits and overlapping rent. The realistic first-year cash outlay beyond the down payment runs $20,000-$30,000. Buyers who drain every dollar for the down payment end up financing the move-in on credit cards at 24% - the most expensive possible way to furnish a house.

Apply the investor's 1% rule as a sanity check even if you are buying to live in it. If a $363,700 home could not plausibly rent for $3,637/month - roughly 1% of its price - you are likely overpaying relative to local income fundamentals, or the market is overheated. In 2026, most U.S. markets fail the 1% test badly (typical ratios run 0.5-0.7%), which does not mean "don't buy" - it means go in clear-eyed that you are buying a home, not an investment, and the affordability math must work on lifestyle terms alone.

Affordability Rules of Thumb

  1. 3-5× income: A $120K earner can typically afford $360K-$600K depending on debts and down payment.
  2. 20% down avoids PMI and lowers the payment, but waiting years to save it while prices rise can cost more than PMI.
  3. Emergency fund first: Keep 3-6 months of the new housing payment saved beyond down payment and closing costs.
  4. Stress-test: Can you afford the payment if rates rise 2% before you buy, or if one income drops 20%? If not, lower your target.

Dual-income qualification hides a fragility the ratios never show. A couple earning $120,000 jointly qualifies for far more than either earns alone - but if one partner loses a job or steps back for childcare, the $2,800 payment must be carried on $60,000-$70,000. Stress-test every purchase against the lower single income: can one of you carry the PITI payment for six months on unemployment plus savings? If the answer is no, either buy 15-20% below your approval amount or build a 9-month emergency fund before closing. Divorce and job loss, not rate moves, cause most distressed sales.

Age and income trajectory should bend the 3-5x rule. A 28-year-old in a high-growth career buying at 4.5x current income is making a reasonable bet that raises will shrink the ratio to 3x within five years - historically the pattern for professionals. A 55-year-old at peak earnings buying at 5x income has no such tailwind and faces retirement within a decade. Younger buyers can lean toward the top of the range; buyers within 15 years of retirement should anchor to the bottom, keeping the payment serviceable on projected retirement income.
