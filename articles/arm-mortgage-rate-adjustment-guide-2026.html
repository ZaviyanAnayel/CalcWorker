ARM Anatomy: Index, Margin, Caps

An adjustable-rate mortgage has four moving parts. The index is a benchmark rate (most 2026 ARMs use the SOFR index). The margin is the lender's fixed markup, typically 2.25% to 3.00%. Your fully indexed rate = index + margin.

New Rate = Current Index Value + Margin (subject to caps)

Caps limit how far the rate can move. A 5/1 ARM quoted as "2/2/5" means: max 2% increase at first adjustment, max 2% per later adjustment, max 5% lifetime increase over the start rate.

The "5/1" means the rate is fixed for 5 years, then adjusts annually. Common structures in 2026: 5/1, 7/1, and 10/1 ARMs.

You can estimate your own fully indexed rate right now, and every ARM borrower should. Pull the current 30-day average SOFR - through 2024-2025 it hovered above 5% at times, and even in 2026 it remains well above the near-zero levels of 2021. Add your margin: SOFR at 4.60% plus a 2.75% margin gives a fully indexed rate of 7.35%. Compare that to your start rate. If the gap exceeds your first-adjustment cap, you already know the cap - not the index - will set your first reset payment, which removes one layer of uncertainty from your planning.

Two cap nuances trip up borrowers. First, caps limit the rate, not the payment - on standard ARMs the payment is recalculated from the new rate, so a 2% rate jump can still mean a 15-20% payment jump. Second, many ARMs carry a floor equal to the margin, meaning your rate can never fall below, say, 2.75% no matter how far the index drops. And watch for "5/2/5" versus "2/2/5" structures: that first number is the initial adjustment cap, and a 5% first-adjustment cap exposes you to dramatically more payment shock than a 2% one.

How a Rate Reset Changes Your Payment

Assume a 5/1 ARM: $450,000 loan, start rate 5.75% fixed for 5 years, margin 2.75%, 2/2/5 caps, SOFR index.

PeriodIndex (SOFR)RateMonthly P&I
Years 1-5 (fixed)—5.75%$2,626
Year 6 (1st adjust)4.30%7.05% (capped at 7.75%)$2,975
Year 7 (mild drop)3.60%6.35%$2,791
Worst case (lifetime cap)—10.75%$4,012

Key insight: the worst-case payment ($4,012) is 53% higher than the start payment. Never take an ARM unless you can afford the lifetime-cap payment or have a firm exit plan (sale or refinance) before the first adjustment.

Here is what actually happens inside the servicer's system at reset: your payment is re-amortized over the remaining term using the new rate and your current balance. On our $450,000 example, after five years at 5.75% the balance is roughly $415,000. The year-6 payment of $2,975 is computed by amortizing $415,000 over the remaining 300 months at 7.05%. This re-amortization is why the payment jump is slightly smaller than a naive rate-ratio calculation suggests - you are spreading payments over fewer months on a smaller balance - but it also means extra principal you paid during the fixed period directly shrinks every future adjusted payment.

Your legal timeline matters as much as the math. Servicers must send an adjustment notice 60 to 120 days before the new payment takes effect, showing the new rate, payment, and index value used. Mark your calendar for 12 months before the first adjustment, not the notice date: that is when you should be collecting refinance quotes and watching the index trend. Borrowers holding 2021-vintage 5/1 ARMs with start rates near 3% are a live 2026 case study - many are resetting toward 7%+, turning a $2,390 payment on $450,000 into roughly $2,990 overnight.

Worked Example: ARM vs Fixed Breakeven

Scenario: $450,000 loan. Option A: 30-year fixed at 6.75% ($2,919/mo). Option B: 7/1 ARM at 5.99% ($2,695/mo), 2/2/5 caps.

Years 1-7: ARM saves $224/month × 84 months = $18,816.

Year 8 adjustment: Suppose SOFR rises and the rate jumps to 7.99% (first-adjust cap). New payment ≈ $3,290 — now $371/month MORE than the fixed loan.

Breakeven: $18,816 ÷ $371 ≈ 51 months. If you sell or refinance within about 11 years total, the ARM wins. Beyond that, the fixed loan wins.

Rule of thumb: an ARM makes sense when your time horizon is clearly shorter than the fixed period, or when you expect rates to fall and plan to refinance.

The breakeven math deserves a sensitivity check, because the single scenario in the original section assumes the worst plausible adjustment. Suppose SOFR cooperates and the year-8 rate lands at 7.0% instead of 7.99%: the new payment is about $3,120, only $201 above the fixed loan's $2,919, and breakeven stretches from 51 months to roughly 94 months - nearly 8 more years of ARM advantage. Conversely, if the index spikes and the lifetime cap binds early, the ARM loses by year 9. Build a three-scenario table (mild, base, severe) before signing; if the ARM only wins in the mild case, the risk premium is not worth it.

Do not forget the escape hatch cost. The "sell or refinance by year 11" plan assumes refinancing is cheap and available. A refinance in year 7 typically costs $4,000-$6,000 in closing costs and requires you to still qualify - with documented income, an appraisal, and a cooperative market. Subtract $5,000 from the $18,816 in fixed-period savings and the true ARM edge is closer to $13,800. If your exit plan depends on refinancing, price that refinance into the comparison on day one.

When an ARM Is (and Isn't) Right for You

Consider an ARM when: you will definitely sell or relocate before the first adjustment; you expect a large income jump; rate forecasts favor declines and you will refinance; the fixed-period savings are substantial (0.75%+).

Avoid an ARM when: this is your "forever home"; your budget cannot absorb the lifetime-cap payment; you have an unstable income; the start-rate discount vs fixed is tiny (under 0.5%).

2026 note: With the Fed funds rate elevated through 2025, many borrowers who took 2021-era ARMs are now facing first adjustments. If that is you, get refinance quotes 6-12 months before your reset date — not after.

Jumbo ARMs deserve special attention because the discount math is far more compelling at scale. While conforming ARM discounts run 0.5-0.75% under fixed rates, jumbo 7/1 and 10/1 ARMs frequently price a full 1.0-1.25% below jumbo fixed rates as banks compete for high-balance borrowers. On a $1,000,000 loan, a 1% discount saves about $640/month - over $53,000 across a 7-year fixed period. For relocating executives and growing families who will certainly move within the fixed window, the jumbo ARM is often the mathematically dominant choice, not a gamble.

One more tool for ARM holders facing a painful reset: recasting after a lump-sum payment. If you receive a $50,000 bonus in year 5, applying it to principal and recasting re-amortizes the remaining balance over the remaining term, permanently lowering every future adjusted payment. On a $415,000 balance at 7.05%, a $50,000 curtailment plus recast cuts the payment by roughly $350/month. It is not as powerful as refinancing into a lower rate, but it requires no appraisal, no income docs, and a fee of only a few hundred dollars.
