The 26-Payment Trick

A biweekly plan splits your monthly payment in half and collects it every two weeks: 26 half-payments per year. Since 26 ÷ 2 = 13, you effectively make 13 full monthly payments instead of 12 — one extra payment yearly, applied straight to principal.

Biweekly Payment = Monthly P&I ÷ 2, paid 26× per year = 13 monthly payments

On a $350,000 loan at 6.75% ($2,269/month), the biweekly amount is $1,134.50 every two weeks. That extra annual payment of $2,269 goes entirely to principal.

The calendar mechanics create two "triple-payment" months each year that catch budgeters off guard. Because there are 26 biweekly periods, two months annually contain three debits instead of two - typically months like January and July, though it shifts yearly. If you are paid biweekly yourself, these align with your three-paycheck months and feel painless. But if you are paid twice monthly (24 paychecks) or monthly, those triple-debit months demand an extra $1,134 you did not budget. Map the debit dates for the full year before enrolling, especially on a tight budget.

Do not confuse biweekly acceleration with a 15-year loan - the economics differ sharply. Our biweekly plan pays off in ~24.5 years; a true 15-year loan at 6.0% on $350,000 demands $2,953/month and finishes with only ~$181,500 in total interest versus biweekly's ~$371,200. The 15-year loan saves nearly $190,000 more in interest but requires $684/month more cash flow every single month. Biweekly is the middle path: meaningful savings with an escape hatch, since you can revert to monthly payments anytime without refinancing.

Interest-rate level dramatically changes the payoff of this strategy, so calibrate expectations. At 3% rates, biweekly acceleration on $350,000 saves only about $25,000 and 3.5 years - nice, but modest. At 6.75% it saves ~$96,000 and 5.5 years. At 8.5%, savings approach $140,000 with 6+ years shaved off. The intuition: extra principal payments destroy future interest, and there is simply more future interest to destroy at higher rates. In 2026's elevated-rate environment, biweekly and accelerated-payment strategies are more valuable than at any point since the 2000s - one more reason to revisit them now.

Front-loading makes the first extra payments the most valuable ones you will ever make. The extra $2,269 principal payment in year 1 eliminates interest on that $2,269 for 29 remaining years - about $4,400 in avoided interest at 6.75%. The same $2,269 extra in year 25 avoids only about $900. If you can only sustain accelerated payments for a few years - say during a high-earning stretch - do it at the start of the loan. Five years of biweekly payments followed by reverting to monthly still captures the majority of the lifetime savings.

DIY Biweekly: Free vs Paid Programs

Warning: Many lenders and third parties charge $300-$500 setup plus monthly fees for "biweekly programs." You do not need them.

Free DIY method: Divide your monthly P&I by 12 and add that amount to each monthly payment. On the $2,269 example: $2,269 ÷ 12 = $189. Add $189 to each payment → $2,458/month. Mathematically identical to biweekly, with zero fees.

Critical: Confirm extra amounts apply to principal, not escrow or future payments. Put "apply to principal" in writing with your servicer.

Servicer suspense accounts are the technical trap behind the "apply to principal" warning. When you send a biweekly half-payment of $1,134.50 but your full monthly payment is $2,269, some servicers cannot apply a partial payment to the loan at all - their systems require the full contractual amount. The money sits in an unapplied "suspense" account earning you nothing until the second half-payment arrives. With certain servicers this works fine; with others, payments have sat unapplied for weeks. Test the arrangement for two months and verify on your statements that each pair of debits reduced principal correctly.

The cleanest DIY setup avoids the issue entirely: keep your regular monthly autopay untouched, then schedule a separate automatic transfer for the extra principal amount - dated 2-3 days after your mortgage payment - labeled explicitly as principal-only curtailment. On the $2,269 example, that is $189/month on autopilot. This sidesteps suspense-account problems, gives you a clear paper trail, and can be paused in one click during tight months - flexibility no third-party biweekly program offers, especially the ones charging $35/month in "service fees."

When Biweekly Doesn't Make Sense

  1. Tight budget: In the two 3-paycheck months, biweekly debits take an extra half-payment. If cash flow is tight, the DIY monthly-addition method is gentler.
  2. Higher-rate debt exists: Credit card debt at 24% destroys wealth faster than mortgage interest at 6.75%. Kill that first.
  3. No emergency fund: Extra mortgage payments are illiquid. Keep 3-6 months of expenses accessible before accelerating.
  4. Prepayment penalty: Rare in 2026, but verify — some non-QM loans still carry them.

The 401(k) match test settles the opportunity-cost question for most borrowers. Diverting $2,269/year to extra mortgage payments saves roughly 6.75% in avoided interest. Putting that same $2,269 into a 401(k) with a 50% employer match instantly becomes $3,404 - a 50% immediate return that no mortgage acceleration can touch. The hierarchy is firm: capture the full employer match first, kill credit card debt second, build the emergency fund third, and only then accelerate the mortgage. Skipping the match to pay a 6.75% mortgage faster is lighting money on fire at a 43-point spread.

One scenario flips the "don't accelerate" advice: private mortgage insurance. On a 5%-down $400,000 loan, PMI of ~$158/month persists until 20% equity - about 7 years on the normal schedule. Biweekly-style extra payments of ~$210/month can pull the 80% LTV date forward by 12-18 months, saving $1,900-$2,800 in PMI premiums on top of the interest savings. When PMI is in play, extra principal payments earn a double return - interest avoided plus insurance premiums eliminated - and should jump ahead of taxable investing in your priority list.
