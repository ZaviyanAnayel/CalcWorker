The Break-Even Formula

Break-Even Units = Fixed Costs / (Price - Variable Cost per Unit)
Break-Even Revenue = Fixed Costs / Contribution Margin Ratio

Coffee shop: Fixed $8,000/mo (rent, salaries), $4 latte price, $1 variable cost (beans, cup).

Contribution margin = $3/cup. Break-even = 8,000 / 3 = 2,667 cups/month (~89/day). Below that: losses. Above: $3 profit per cup.

The dollar version of the formula serves the P&L while units serve operations. Coffee shop: $8,000 fixed costs divided by the contribution margin ratio (3/4 = 0.75) gives $10,667 in break-even revenue. Cross-check: 2,667 cups x $4 = $10,668. Same answer, two lenses - use units for staffing and inventory, dollars for financial planning.

Multi-product businesses weight the margin. Product A contributes $3 (60% of sales), B contributes $5 (40%): weighted contribution = 0.6 x 3 + 0.4 x 5 = $3.80 per blended unit. Break-even = 8,000/3.80, about 2,106 blended units. Shifting the mix toward B lowers the threshold - product mix is a break-even lever most owners never pull.

Fixed vs Variable Costs

Fixed (don't change with sales)Variable (scale with sales)
Rent, insuranceMaterials, ingredients
SalariesCommissions
Software subscriptionsShipping, packaging
Loan paymentsTransaction fees

Classify carefully: Misclassifying a step-cost (like hiring when busy) distorts the analysis. Revisit quarterly.

Some costs live between the categories. Electricity at $200 base plus $0.50 per unit is semi-variable - part fixed, part scaling. A supervisor required per 50 workers is a step cost: fixed until headcount crosses the step, then it jumps. Classify by behavior at your current volume, and split semi-variable costs into their fixed and variable pieces before running the formula.

Misclassification quietly corrupts the analysis. Treating overtime-heavy "salaried" labor as fixed when it really scales can understate variable cost by $0.40 per unit - across 10,000 units that is $4,000 of phantom margin and a break-even point set dangerously low. Revisit every classification quarterly; businesses change shape faster than their cost labels.

SaaS Break-Even Example

Startup: Fixed $50,000/mo (team, servers), $99/mo per customer, $9 variable (support, hosting).

  • Contribution margin: $90/customer
  • Break-even: 50,000 / 90 = 556 customers
  • At 800 customers: profit = (800 - 556) x 90 = $21,960/mo
  • Each customer beyond 556 is nearly pure contribution

This is why SaaS scales beautifully - high fixed costs, tiny variable costs, explosive post-break-even profits.

Payback period is break-even's dynamic twin. With $450 customer acquisition cost and $90 monthly contribution, payback is 450/90 = 5 months. At a 36-month average customer life, lifetime value is $3,240 and LTV:CAC = 7.2 - elite territory. Break-even counts customers; payback counts months; together they say whether growth funds itself.

Churn moves the goalpost. At 5% monthly churn the average customer lasts 20 months; if CAC payback stretches past customer lifetime, the business loses money on every cohort no matter how many customers it signs. No customer count fixes broken unit economics - the break-even formula assumes each unit contributes, and churned units stop contributing.

Lowering Your Break-Even Point

  1. Cut fixed costs - renegotiate rent, go remote, refinance debt.
  2. Raise prices - $4 to $4.50 latte: break-even drops to 8,000/3.5 = 2,286 cups.
  3. Reduce variable costs - supplier bids, waste reduction.
  4. Shift mix - push high-contribution products.
  5. Add revenue streams - catering, merchandise, subscriptions.

Price hikes can backfire through elasticity. Raising the latte from $4 to $4.50 drops break-even to 2,286 cups - but if volume falls 20% (2,667 to 2,134 cups), you land below the new threshold and worse off than before. The formula holds price constant while you change it; model the volume response or the "improvement" is fiction.

Swapping fixed costs for variable ones trades break-even level for risk. Replace a $5,000/month salary with a $2/unit contractor: fixed falls to $3,000 but contribution shrinks to $4 - $3 = $1, so break-even rises to 3,000 cups. Worse threshold - yet at 1,000 slow-month cups the loss is only $2,000 versus $5,000 before. Higher break-even, smaller disasters: that is the real tradeoff.
