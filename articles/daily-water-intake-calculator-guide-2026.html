The Weight-Based Formula

Daily Water (oz) = Body Weight (lb) / 2 | Daily Water (L) = Weight (kg) x 0.033

180 lb person: 90 oz = about 2.7 liters / 11 cups. This covers total water - about 20% typically comes from food (fruits, vegetables, soups), so ~9 cups of fluids is the practical target.

The old "8x8" rule (8 cups of 8 oz) undershoots larger active people and overshoots small sedentary ones - weight-based beats one-size-fits-all.

Metric walkthrough: 70 kg x 0.033 = 2.31 L total; ~20% (460 ml) arrives via food — fruits, vegetables, soups — leaving ~1.85 L to drink, about 7-8 cups. Add 300 ml in pregnancy and 700 ml while breastfeeding (breast milk is 87% water). A 50-kg sedentary woman needs just 1.65 L total — the "3 liters for everyone" advice overshoots her by nearly double, which is why weight-based math exists.

Special populations invert the rules: children 4-8 need ~1.2-1.7 L/day; adults over 65 lose thirst sensitivity and should drink on schedule rather than thirst — dehydration hospitalizes more elderly each summer than heatstroke does. Kidney disease reverses everything: fluid may be restricted to 1-1.5 L/day by doctor's order, and "drink more water" becomes genuinely dangerous advice.

Adjusting for Activity and Climate

  • Exercise: +12-16 oz per 30 minutes of moderate activity; +20-32 oz per hour of intense/sweaty training.
  • Heat: Hot climates or heat waves add 16-32 oz/day.
  • Altitude: Above 8,000 ft, add ~16 oz/day (faster breathing losses).
  • Illness: Fever, vomiting, or diarrhea demand aggressive rehydration with electrolytes.

Example: 180 lb runner, 3-hour race in heat: base 90 oz + ~60 oz exercise + 16 oz heat = about 166 oz (1.3 gallons) for the day.

Measure your sweat rate like a lab: weigh nude before and after a 1-hour run; each kilogram lost = 1 liter. Example: lose 1.2 kg, drank 500 ml → sweat rate 1.7 L/hr. Replace ~150% of losses in the hours after (the extra covers ongoing urine loss) — that runner needs ~2.5 L post-run, with sodium, not just water. Repeat the test in summer heat; most runners' rates jump 30-50%.

Sodium numbers: sweat carries 500-1,500 mg sodium per liter — salty sweaters hit the top end, and white crust on dark clothes is the tell. Most sports drinks supply only 300-600 mg/L, so cramp-prone athletes often need 1,000+ mg/hr from salt capsules or electrolyte mixes. Plain water for efforts over 2 hours dilutes blood sodium — marathon hyponatremia cases are almost always overdrinkers, not dehydrated runners.

Dehydration Signs and Overhydration

Dehydrated: dark yellow urine, headache, fatigue, dizziness. Just 2% body weight lost = measurable performance drop; 3%+ = serious.

The urine test: Pale straw = hydrated. Clear = possibly overdoing it. Dark amber = drink now.

Overhydration (hyponatremia): Drinking far beyond thirst during endurance events dilutes blood sodium - nausea, confusion, seizures. Rare but dangerous. Drink to thirst plus a bit, and use electrolytes (not just water) for efforts over 2 hours.

Performance cost, quantified: 2% body-weight fluid loss cuts endurance 10-20% and raises heart rate ~8 bpm at the same pace; just 1-2% impairs concentration, short-term memory, and mood — the "afternoon slump" is often dehydration plus lunch. Heat illness escalates past 3%: cramps → exhaustion → stroke, with core temperatures over 104°F becoming a medical emergency. In summer races, the aid stations matter more than the pace plan.

Hyponatremia math: drinking 1.5 L/hr across a 5-hour marathon = 7.5 L against ~4-5 L of sweat loss — the surplus dilutes blood sodium below 135 mmol/L, causing nausea, confusion, and in rare cases seizures. Prevention is simple: drink to thirst, take electrolytes, and don't gain weight during the race — finish-line weigh-ins catching 2-4% gains flag the overdrinkers, who are at higher risk than the slightly dehydrated.

Practical Hydration Habits

  1. Front-load: 16-20 oz within an hour of waking.
  2. Keep water visible - desk bottle, car bottle.
  3. One glass per caffeinated drink (coffee's diuretic effect is mild, but the habit helps).
  4. Eat your water: cucumber, watermelon, oranges are 85-95% water.
  5. Limit alcohol - it suppresses vasopressin, increasing fluid loss.

Container math beats cup-counting: a 32-oz bottle emptied 3 times = 96 oz — most people hit their target with "bottle accounting" better than tallying cups. Habit-stack it: one glass with each meal (24 oz), one on waking (16 oz), one mid-afternoon (16 oz) = 56 oz before any conscious effort. Phone reminders every 2 hours work for the first 3 weeks; after that the habit carries itself.

What counts: coffee and tea hydrate at ~80-90% of water's value (200 mg caffeine causes only mild diuresis in habitual drinkers); milk and juice count fully but carry calories; alcohol is negative — pair each drink with a full glass of water. Sparkling water hydrates identically to still — the "it doesn't count" claim is pure myth, carbonation changes nothing about absorption.
