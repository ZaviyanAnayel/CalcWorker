Protein Targets by Goal

Daily Protein (g) = Body Weight (lb) x Factor
GoalFactor180 lb Example
Sedentary adult0.5-0.690-108g
Active / maintenance0.7-0.8126-144g
Muscle building0.8-1.0144-180g
Fat loss (dieting)1.0-1.2180-216g

Use goal body weight if significantly overweight - protein needs scale with lean mass, not fat.

Metric targets: muscle building 1.6-2.2 g/kg, cutting 2.0-2.7 g/kg. An 82-kg lifter needs 131-180g building, 164-221g cutting. The RDA of 0.8 g/kg (56g for a 70-kg adult) is the minimum to avoid deficiency, not an optimal target — it was set from nitrogen-balance studies on sedentary young men, a population that describes almost nobody reading this guide. Treat the RDA as the floor of the building, not the penthouse.

Older adults need more, not less: 1.2-1.6 g/kg fights sarcopenia — a 70-kg 70-year-old needs 84-112g vs the 56g RDA, nearly double. Illness, surgery recovery, and wound healing push needs to 1.5-2.0 g/kg. The "seniors should eat light" instinct accelerates the exact muscle loss behind frailty, falls, and hip fractures — protein is fall prevention you can eat.

Per-Meal Distribution

Muscle protein synthesis maxes around 30-50g per meal. Front-loading all protein at dinner wastes the opportunity:

  • 180g target: 45g x 4 meals (breakfast, lunch, snack, dinner)
  • Breakfast is the typical failure point - most people eat 10-15g; aim for 30g+ (Greek yogurt + eggs).
  • Pre-sleep protein (30-40g casein or cottage cheese) modestly boosts overnight recovery.

Spacing science: muscle protein synthesis stays elevated ~3-5 hours after a protein meal, then goes refractory — 4 x 40g across the day stimulates ~25% more total daily synthesis than 2 x 80g in trials. The practical layout: breakfast, lunch, post-workout/dinner, pre-sleep. The pre-sleep 30-40g casein feeding measurably improves overnight recovery in lifters — slow-digesting protein while growth hormone peaks is a legitimate free win.

Snack math that fixes the day: swap a 4g-protein granola bar for 200g Greek yogurt (+16g), add 100g cottage cheese (+11g), or 1 oz jerky (+9-15g) — three swaps add 35-40g without a single extra meal. Watch the liquid leak: a 500-cal latte with 8g protein vs a 160-cal shake with 25g occupy the same habit slot with triple the protein and a third of the calories.

Cost per 25g protein (US 2026 averages): dried lentils ~$0.25-0.40, eggs ~$0.50-0.70, whey ~$0.75-1.00, chicken breast ~$1.00-1.50, Greek yogurt ~$1.00-1.25, fresh salmon ~$3-4. Budget royalty: lentils, eggs, milk, peanut butter. A 150g/day target costs ~$3-5 from the cheap end — protein is not inherently expensive; steak is. The broke-lifter staples (eggs, whey, lentils) are also the nutritional best buys.

Quality scoring: whey, eggs, and milk score 1.0 on PDCAAS/DIAAS; soy 0.9-1.0; pea 0.6-0.7; wheat 0.4-0.5. Plant eaters close the gap two ways: 10-15% higher total targets plus combining sources — rice + beans completes the amino profile, and the old "same meal" rule is outdated; same day suffices. Leucine is usually the limiting factor: eat more total or add 5g straight.

Protein Myths

  • Kidney damage: No evidence in healthy people up to 2g/lb. (Pre-existing kidney disease is different - consult a doctor.)
  • More is always better: Benefits plateau around 0.8-1.0g/lb for muscle; excess just becomes expensive calories.
  • Plant protein is useless: Lower leucine/digestibility, but 10-15% higher targets and variety close the gap.
  • Timing window: The "30-minute anabolic window" is overstated - total daily intake dominates.

The kidney evidence, directly: a 1-year trial at 2.5 g/kg showed no decline in GFR or kidney markers in healthy adults. The myth descends from kidney-disease patients, where protein is restricted — healthy kidneys aren't damaged by filtering more, they're built for it. The genuine caveat: diagnosed CKD, single kidney, or diabetes-related kidney stress means dosing with your nephrologist, not a forum — exceptions exist, they just aren't you.

"Excess protein turns to fat" — true but misleading: overeating protein in a surplus still stores fat, yet protein-overfeeding trials show far less fat gain than carb/fat overfeeding at equal calories (thermic effect plus satiety blunt the surplus). And the "protein causes dangerous ketones" confusion: dietary protein doesn't cause ketoacidosis — that's an insulin-deficiency emergency, physiologically unrelated to your chicken breast.
