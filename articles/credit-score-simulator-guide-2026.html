1. Why Your Score Is a Formula, Not a Mystery

Your credit score feels opaque, but the dominant model — FICO — publishes exactly how much each behavior weighs. Scores run 300 to 850, and every point you gain or lose has a dollar value, because lenders translate scores into interest rates. A 40-point swing can change a mortgage rate by half a point or more, costing tens of thousands over 30 years.

A credit score simulator applies those published weights so you can preview, before you act, roughly how a decision — paying down a card, opening a new account, or missing a payment — will move your score. It's a planning tool, not a crystal ball: bureaus use proprietary versions with dozens of sub-factors, so the value is in direction and magnitude, not exact points.

2. The Five FICO Factors & Their Weights

Payment history (35%) + Amounts owed / utilization (30%) + Length of history (15%)
+ New credit (10%) + Credit mix (10%) = 100%
  • Payment history (35%): the single biggest factor. One 30-day late payment can drop a good score 60–110 points. This is why "pay on time, always" is the whole ballgame.
  • Amounts owed / utilization (30%): your balances divided by your credit limits. Under 10% is ideal; above 30% starts to hurt. Crucially, utilization has no memory — it resets every month your new balances report.
  • Length of history (15%): the average age of your accounts. Keep old cards open even if you don't use them.
  • New credit (10%): hard inquiries and brand-new accounts shave a few to ~12 points each.
  • Credit mix (10%): a blend of revolving (cards) and installment (auto, mortgage) accounts helps slightly.

The leverage is concentrated at the top: payment history and utilization together are 65% of your score, and both are within your direct control. That's the practical takeaway — master those two and the other three handle themselves.

3. Worked Example: A 720 Score

Start with a 720 score, 100% on-time payment history, 20% utilization, no missed payments, one new account in the last year, and 8 years of credit history:

  1. Utilization effect: running 20% instead of the sub-10% ideal costs roughly −18 points.
  2. New-account effect: one recent account costs roughly −12 points.
  3. Simulated score: 720 − 18 − 12 = 690.
  4. The lever: pay utilization down to 8% and the simulation rebounds to roughly 712 — because utilization resets monthly.

This is why utilization is the fastest lever most people can pull. A missed payment, by contrast, doesn't just subtract — it reclassifies your "payment history" bucket and lingers for seven years. 💡 Strategic Pro Tip:
Never close your oldest card before a mortgage application. Closing it shortens your average account age (15% of the score) and, by removing available credit, can spike your utilization (30%) — a double hit when it matters most.

4. Timing Tricks Most People Miss

Statement Date vs. Due Date

Card issuers report your balance on the statement closing date, not the due date. If you pay in full after the statement closes but before the due date, the high balance has already been reported. To show low utilization, pay your balance down before the statement posts. This one habit can add 10–20 points with no other change.

Rate-Shopping Windows

Multiple hard inquiries for the same loan type within a 14–45 day window are treated as a single inquiry by FICO scoring. Shop aggressively in a short burst rather than spreading applications over months. Our DTI calculator helps you see whether the loan you're shopping for will even qualify.

FICO vs. VantageScore

Most lenders use FICO — for mortgages, often an older FICO version. The free "VantageScore" your bank app shows can differ by 20–40 points from the score a mortgage underwriter sees. Treat free scores as directional. Whatever your number, model the payment at several rates to see what each score tier actually buys.

5. What Your Score Costs on a Mortgage

Lenders price in score tiers. Borrowers above ~760 get the best rates, with meaningful steps down at 740, 700, 680, and 620 (the conventional floor). On a $400,000 30-year loan at 2026 rates, the gap between a 620 and a 760 score can exceed $300/month — over $100,000 across the life of the loan.
