Blood Pressure Categories

CategorySystolicDiastolic
NormalUnder 120Under 80
Elevated120-129Under 80
Stage 1 hypertension130-13980-89
Stage 2 hypertension140+90+
Hypertensive crisisOver 180Over 120

Example: 138/86 = Stage 1. Either number reaching a higher category determines the stage. A crisis reading with symptoms (chest pain, numbness) needs emergency care.

Isolated systolic hypertension — say 150/75 in a 70-year-old — is the classic elderly pattern: stiff arteries raise systolic while diastolic stays low or even falls. Pulse pressure (systolic minus diastolic) over 60 mmHg independently predicts risk; that 150/75 gives 75, a red flag hiding behind a "normal" diastolic. Treatment targets for over-65: under 130/80 if tolerated, but avoid pushing diastolic under 70 — coronary arteries fill during diastole.

White-coat effect adds 10-20 mmHg in the office for ~20% of patients — home readings of 135/85 with office 155/95 is white-coat, not true hypertension. Masked hypertension is the reverse and more dangerous: normal in the office, high at home, missed entirely by clinic-only care. The tiebreaker is 24-hour ambulatory monitoring — the gold standard — with daytime average under 130/80.

What Raises Blood Pressure

  • Sodium: Average intake ~3,400mg vs recommended under 2,300mg (ideally 1,500mg).
  • Weight: Each 2 lb lost drops systolic ~1 mmHg.
  • Alcohol: 3+ drinks/day significantly raises BP.
  • Inactivity, smoking, stress, poor sleep all contribute.
  • Medications: NSAIDs, decongestants, some antidepressants raise BP - check yours.

Salt sensitivity: 30-50% of hypertensives (higher in older adults) see blood pressure track sodium closely. One restaurant meal delivers 2,000-4,000 mg; bread and rolls — not the salt shaker — are the #1 sodium source in Western diets. Cutting to 1,500 mg typically drops systolic 5-6 mmHg in sensitive individuals. Read the bread label before blaming the fries: two slices can carry 300-400 mg before anything is spread on them.

Sleep apnea is the hidden driver: ~50% of apnea patients are hypertensive, and severe apnea raises resistant-hypertension risk 2-3x. CPAP treatment drops BP 5-10 mmHg in compliant users — comparable to a medication. Red flags: loud nightly snoring, witnessed apneas, morning headaches, daytime sleepiness. A sleep study is as much a BP intervention as a diet change for these patients.

Lowering It: What Actually Moves Numbers

Combined example: 145/92 + DASH + 10 lb loss + exercise could reach about 125/80 - from Stage 2 to elevated, no medication. Lifestyle is first-line treatment for Stage 1.

Potassium is sodium's counterweight: 3,500-5,000 mg/day lowers systolic 4-5 mmHg. Food math: banana 420 mg, medium potato 900 mg, cup of beans 600-800 mg, avocado 700 mg. The DASH servings that deliver it: 4-5 fruit/vegetable servings plus 2-3 low-fat dairy daily. One caution that flips the advice: kidney disease means potassium restriction — check with your doctor before loading up if your kidneys are compromised.

The surprise winner in 2023 meta-analyses: isometric exercise — wall sits or handgrip holds, 4 x 2 minutes, 3x/week — dropped systolic 8-10 mmHg, outperforming aerobic training head-to-head. Slow breathing (6 breaths/min, device-guided, 15 min/day) adds another 3-5. Stack DASH + isometrics + sodium control and a Stage 1 reader can move 15+ mmHg without a prescription — lifestyle as first-line therapy, quantified.

Home Monitoring Done Right

  1. Use a validated upper-arm cuff (wrist cuffs are less accurate).
  2. Sit 5 minutes, feet flat, back supported, arm at heart level.
  3. No caffeine, exercise, or smoking 30 min before.
  4. Take 2 readings 1-2 min apart; record the average.
  5. Measure same times daily (morning + evening) for a week; share the log with your doctor.

Cuff size is the #1 home error: the bladder should encircle ~40% of arm circumference — a too-small cuff on a 16" arm overreads systolic 5-15 mmHg, manufacturing fake hypertension. Measure your mid-upper arm: under 13" = standard cuff, 13-17" = large. Position the cuff's artery marker over the brachial artery with its bottom edge 1" above the elbow crease; a cuff riding up or twisted reads high.

The 7-day diagnostic protocol: measure morning and evening (2 readings each, 1 min apart), discard day 1 (anxiety-inflated), average the remaining ~24 readings — that average, not any single spike, guides treatment. Log it in a simple table or the cuff's app and bring the printout; doctors now titrate medication off home averages, and validated-device lists (STRIDE BP, US ValidateBP) beat Amazon star ratings for choosing hardware.
