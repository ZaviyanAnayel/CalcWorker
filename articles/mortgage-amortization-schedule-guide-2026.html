How Amortization Works: The Monthly Payment Formula

Every fixed-rate mortgage payment is split into two parts: interest owed to the lender and principal that reduces your loan balance. Early in the loan, interest dominates; late in the loan, principal dominates. This shifting split is called amortization.

M = P × [r(1+r)n] / [(1+r)n − 1]

Where M is the monthly payment, P is the loan principal, r is the monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12), and n is the total number of payments. For a $400,000 loan at 6.5% over 30 years (360 payments), r = 0.005417, and the monthly payment is $2,528.27.

Each month, the lender first takes its interest cut: balance × monthly rate. Whatever remains of your payment reduces principal. Because the balance shrinks slowly at first, the interest portion stays high for years.

One detail that surprises first-time buyers: your very first payment's interest portion is slightly different from the formula's clean math because interest accrues from your closing date, not the first of the month. If you close on the 12th of a 30-day month, you prepay about 18 days of "stub" interest at closing - roughly $1,285 on a $400,000 loan at 6.5% - and none of it reduces principal. Closing near month-end minimizes this prepaid interest, which is why savvy buyers and lenders often target the last week of the month.

Term length changes the amortization picture far more than most borrowers expect. That same $400,000 loan at 6.25% over 15 years carries a $3,430 monthly payment but only about $217,400 in total interest, versus roughly $510,000 over 30 years. The crossover point - where principal exceeds interest - arrives around month 40 instead of month 228. If the 15-year payment strains your budget, a middle path exists: take the 30-year loan and voluntarily pay the 15-year amount whenever cash flow allows.

In year one, roughly 85% of each payment is interest. You do not reach the crossover point — where principal exceeds interest — until around year 19. Total interest paid over the full 30 years is about $510,178, more than the original loan.

The slow early principal payoff has a brutal implication if you sell in the first five to seven years. After 60 payments totaling about $151,700 on our $400,000 example, the balance is still $373,410 - meaning only $26,590 of your payments actually retired debt. With a 10% down payment, your equity is roughly $66,590, but 6% selling costs on a $400,000 home eat $24,000 of that. This is why buying with plans to move in under five years often loses money even in a flat market: amortization simply has not had time to work for you.

The crossover timing is highly rate-sensitive, which matters in 2026's elevated-rate environment. At 3.5%, principal overtakes interest around year 11; at 6.5% it takes until about year 19; at 8% it stretches past year 23. In other words, higher rates do not just raise your payment - they lengthen the interest-heavy phase of the loan. Borrowers who bought at 2021-era 3% rates and refinance into 2026 rates restart this clock at the worst possible point in the curve.

Worked Example: What Extra Payments Actually Save

Scenario: Same $400,000 loan at 6.5%, 30-year term. You add $200 extra toward principal each month, starting in month 1.

Without extra payments: 360 payments, total interest $510,178, payoff in 30 years.

With $200/month extra:

  • Loan pays off in 24 years and 2 months (about 70 payments early)
  • Total interest drops to roughly $403,900
  • Interest saved: about $106,000

The math works because extra principal payments compound: each dollar of extra principal eliminates all future interest that dollar would have accrued. A one-time $10,000 lump sum in year 3 saves roughly $28,000 in interest over the remaining life of this loan.

Caution: confirm your lender applies extra payments to principal (not as a prepaid future payment) and that no prepayment penalty applies.

Timing of extra payments matters enormously, and this is where most advice goes vague. That $200 monthly extra started in month 1 saves about $106,000 and cuts 70 payments. Start the same $200 in month 121 instead, and you save only around $48,000 while shaving roughly 30 payments - less than half the benefit for the same total outlay of $48,000. Early extra dollars kill the most future interest because they attack the balance when it is largest. If you can only accelerate for a few years, do it in years 1 through 7, not years 15 through 20.

There is also a smarter alternative to blind prepayment once you have a lump sum: recasting. Put $25,000 toward principal in year 4 and ask your servicer to recast (re-amortize) the loan for a $250-$500 fee. Your term stays the same but your required payment drops by roughly $160/month permanently, improving monthly cash flow. Straight prepayment shortens the loan instead. Choose recasting when you want breathing room in your budget; choose pure prepayment when you want the loan gone fastest.

Common Amortization Mistakes to Avoid

  1. Refinancing resets the clock. Refinancing into a new 30-year loan after 8 years restarts the high-interest phase. Compare total interest, not just the monthly payment.
  2. Ignoring the crossover. Selling or refinancing before the crossover point means most of what you paid was interest — your equity is mostly your down payment.
  3. Biweekly confusion. True biweekly plans make 26 half-payments (13 full payments yearly), cutting about 4-5 years off a 30-year loan. Some lenders charge fees for this — you can replicate it free by dividing your payment by 12 and adding that to each monthly payment.
  4. Not requesting an amortization schedule. Your lender must provide one. Review it yearly to track your equity buildup and plan extra payments strategically.

The refinance-reset mistake deserves real numbers because it is the costliest one here. Suppose at year 8 your balance is about $371,000 and you refinance from 6.5% into 6.0% - but into a new 30-year term. Your payment drops from $2,528 to about $2,224, which feels like a win. But you now face 360 fresh payments: total remaining interest of roughly $430,000 versus about $352,000 if you had kept the original loan's remaining 22 years. The correct move is refinancing into a 22-year term (or making the old payment on the new loan), which captures the lower rate without restarting the amortization clock.

A second underappreciated trap: extra payments do not reduce your escrow portion, and with some servicers they are not automatically applied to principal at all. Several large servicers park partial extra amounts in a "suspense account" until they total a full payment, or apply them as early future payments - which saves you zero interest. Always make principal-only payments as a separate transaction labeled "principal curtailment," and confirm on the next statement that the balance dropped by the full extra amount.
