The Cap Rate Formula

Cap Rate = Net Operating Income ÷ Property Value (or Price)

NOI = all rental income minus operating expenses (taxes, insurance, maintenance, vacancy, management) — excluding mortgage payments. Cap rate measures the property's unleveraged return.

Example: $24,000 NOI on a $400,000 property = 6.0% cap rate.

Professionals track three different cap rates per deal. Going-in cap: today's NOI ÷ purchase price — what you're actually buying. Stabilized cap: pro-forma NOI after renovations ÷ all-in cost — what the business plan promises. Exit cap: projected sale-year NOI ÷ future sale price — what the next buyer will pay. The classic value-add: buy at a 5% going-in cap, stabilize to 7% on total cost, sell at a 5.5% exit. The spread between those three numbers is where the profit lives — quote only one and you're guessing.

Reversion math shows why cap-rate shifts are violent. NOI of $20,000 at a 5% exit cap = $400,000 value. If buyers demand 6% instead, value drops to $333,333 — a single percentage point of cap-rate expansion erases 17% of value with zero change in rents. This is the silent killer of syndications bought at 4% caps with floating-rate debt: the asset didn't deteriorate, the denominator just moved.

What Cap Rates Mean

Cap RateTypical Profile
3-5%Prime coastal markets; low risk, appreciation-driven
5-7%Balanced markets; income + moderate growth
8%+Higher risk/tertiary markets; cash flow-driven

High cap rate = higher return and higher risk (weaker tenants, slower appreciation, economic sensitivity). There is no universally "good" cap rate — only appropriate ones for the market and your goals.

The 2026 landscape in broad strokes: coastal primary markets 3.5-4.5%, Sun Belt growth markets 5.5-6.5%, Midwest cash-flow markets 7-9%. An 8% cap in Cleveland and a 4% cap in San Diego are both "good" — they reflect opposite bets: cash flow now versus appreciation later. Judge every cap rate against its submarket comps, never against a national ideal. A 6% cap is a steal in one zip code and a warning sign three miles away.

Cap-rate compression minted fortunes from 2020-2021: buy at a 7% cap ($20,000 NOI = $285,714 value); the market compresses to 5% and the identical NOI prices at $400,000 — a 40% gain with zero rent growth. But 2022-2023 expansion reversed the trade just as mechanically. Cap rates are a market tide; buying at the low-tide (high-cap) end of the cycle is the entire game for appreciation investors.

Duplex Walkthrough

Purchase: $350,000 duplex. Rents: $1,400 + $1,400 = $33,600/year gross.

Cap rate = $18,544 ÷ $350,000 = 5.3%. With 25% down ($87,500) and a $262,500 loan at 7% ($1,746/mo = $20,952/yr), cash flow is negative $2,408/year — a reminder that cap rate ignores financing. Always layer in the mortgage.

Underwrite a triplex for contrast: $420,000 price, $4,200/month gross ($50,400/year). Subtract 8% vacancy ($4,032), taxes $5,400, insurance $2,200, maintenance 8% ($4,032), management 8% ($4,032) → NOI $30,704 → cap rate 7.3%. Higher than the duplex example — smaller markets pay higher caps, but you earn it in management intensity per dollar invested. Three tenants, three water heaters, three times the turnover.

Add the per-unit lens: $30,704 ÷ 3 units = $10,235 NOI per unit per year. Compare per-unit NOI across listings the way you'd compare per-share earnings — the triplex showing $7,000/unit NOI against a market of $10,000 either hides upside (below-market rents you can raise) or problems (deferred maintenance eating the margin). The variance tells you which questions to ask before bidding.

Cap Rate Limitations

  • Ignores financing: Two investors get different cash-on-cash returns from the same cap rate.
  • Snapshot only: Says nothing about rent growth, appreciation, or future capex.
  • Manipulable: Pro-forma rents and understated expenses inflate quoted cap rates — underwrite with your numbers.
  • Market-relative: A 5% cap is great in San Francisco, poor in Cleveland. Compare within the submarket.

Pair cap rate with the gross rent multiplier: $350,000 ÷ $33,600 gross rent = 10.4×. Under ~10× usually signals a cash-flow market; over ~14× signals an appreciation market. GRM's virtue is speed — computable from a listing headline — but it ignores expenses entirely, so a 9× GRM with 60% expense ratios is worse than an 11× with 35%. Use GRM to screen, cap rate to underwrite, never GRM alone to decide.

See the deal through the lender's eyes with DSCR: NOI ÷ annual debt service. Most lenders demand 1.20-1.25×. The duplex example: $18,544 NOI ÷ $20,952 debt service = 0.89× — negative leverage no conventional lender will touch. Cap rate tells you what the asset earns; DSCR tells you whether the bank agrees to fund it. Underwrite both before falling in love with a listing.
