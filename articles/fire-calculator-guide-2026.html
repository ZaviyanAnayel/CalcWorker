1. What FIRE Actually Means

FIRE — Financial Independence, Retire Early — is built on a single, almost mechanical idea: when your invested assets generate enough to cover your living expenses forever, work becomes optional. There's no magic threshold and no lottery ticket involved; it's a function of your spending, your savings rate, and time.

What surprises most people is that the math has nothing to do with income and everything to do with the gap between income and spending. A $200,000 earner who spends $195,000 is further from independence than a $70,000 earner who spends $45,000. Your savings rate — the percentage of income you keep — is the single most powerful variable, because it determines both how fast your portfolio grows and how much you ultimately need.

2. The FIRE Number & the 4% Rule

Your FIRE number is the portfolio size that makes you financially independent. The standard way to derive it is the 4% rule, popularized by the 1998 Trinity Study, which found that withdrawing 4% of a balanced portfolio in year one — then adjusting that dollar amount for inflation each year — survived 30-year retirements across almost every historical market scenario.

FIRE Number = Annual Expenses ÷ Safe Withdrawal Rate

Example: $60,000 ÷ 0.04 = $1,500,000 (i.e., 25 × your annual spending)

Then you project whether your savings will reach that number, using the future value of a growing annuity:

FV = P(1+r)^t + C × [ ((1+r)^t − 1) ÷ r ]

P = current savings | C = annual contribution | r = annual return | t = years

The 4% rule is a heuristic, not a law of physics. For a 30-year retirement it has held up historically; for a 50-year early retirement it deserves a safety margin. The rule also assumes a stock-heavy, low-fee portfolio. Our calculator lets you dial the withdrawal rate down to 3% or lower to reflect that longer horizon.

3. Worked Example: A 35-Year-Old Saver

Meet a 35-year-old with $100,000 invested, adding $20,000/year, earning 7%, and planning to spend $60,000/year in retirement:

  1. FIRE number: $60,000 ÷ 0.04 = $1,500,000.
  2. Growth over 25 years (to age 60): FV = 100,000 × (1.07)^25 + 20,000 × [((1.07)^25 − 1) ÷ 0.07] ≈ $1,807,700.
  3. Result: roughly $307,700 past the FIRE number by age 60.
  4. Solve for the actual crossing point: the portfolio hits $1.5M at about age 57.6 — over two years earlier than planned.

Now stress-test the assumptions. Drop the return to 6% and the FI age slips to roughly 59. Lower the withdrawal rate to 3.5% (raising the target to $1.71M) and it moves past 61. Raise contributions to $30,000/year and FI arrives around 54. These sensitivities are why running the numbers — not guessing — matters. 💡 Strategic Pro Tip:
Cutting expenses is the highest-leverage move in all of FIRE. Trimming $5,000/year of spending lowers your FIRE number by $125,000 (at 4%) and raises your savings rate by the same $5,000. It attacks the problem from both ends at once.

4. Lean, Fat, Coast & Barista FIRE

FIRE is a spectrum, not a single target. Knowing which flavor fits you changes the number you chase:

  • Lean FIRE: retiring on minimal spending, often under $40,000/year — FIRE number around $1,000,000. Low cost of living, but less cushion for healthcare or surprises.
  • Fat FIRE: a $100,000+/year lifestyle — FIRE number $2,500,000+. Comfortable, but demands a much higher savings rate or income for longer.
  • Coast FIRE: save enough early that compounding alone reaches your number by traditional retirement age, then work only to cover current expenses. The classic "front-load and relax" path.
  • Barista FIRE: semi-retire early with part-time work that provides benefits (notably health insurance), letting a smaller portfolio stretch further.

5. Sequence Risk, Taxes & Fees

Sequence-of-Returns Risk

The 4% rule's biggest weakness is sequence risk: a bear market in the first few years of retirement depletes a portfolio far faster than the same decline later, because you're selling assets at the bottom. Mitigations include a larger bond allocation ("bond tent") in the years around retirement, flexible withdrawals (trimming spending 10–20% in down years), and part-time income that reduces drawdowns.

Healthcare Before Medicare

Early retirees must budget health insurance explicitly. Pre-Medicare coverage — ACA marketplace or otherwise — can run $500–$1,500 per month per person depending on age and location. That single line item can raise your FIRE number by six figures.

Fees and Taxes Are Silent Killers

A 1% advisory fee over 40 years can consume nearly a third of your potential wealth. Prefer low-cost index funds, and fill tax-advantaged accounts first. Use our Roth IRA calculator to see how tax-free growth compounds, and track overall progress with the net worth calculator.
