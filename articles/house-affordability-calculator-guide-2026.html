1. Affordability Is a Payment, Not a Price

"How much house can I afford?" is really two questions: how much will a lender approve, and how much can you comfortably carry. They are rarely the same number. Lenders underwrite to your ability to service the payment, so they work backward from your income to a maximum home price. Your own comfort level, maintenance budget, and risk tolerance usually land 10–20% below that ceiling.

In 2026, with 30-year rates near 6.8%, the same income simply buys less house than it did at 3%. A household earning $120,000 that could have carried a $550,000 home at 3.5% now tops out closer to $410,000. Understanding the math keeps you from anchoring to outdated expectations — and from signing up for a payment that strangles your budget.

2. The 28/36 Rule, Solved in Reverse

Conventional underwriting rests on the 28/36 rule:

  • Front-end (28%): your housing payment (PITI) should not exceed 28% of gross monthly income.
  • Back-end (36%): all recurring debts — housing plus car, student, and credit-card minimums — should stay under 36% of gross monthly income.
Housing budget = min( 28% × gross monthly income , 36% × gross monthly income − other monthly debts )

Here's the crucial insight most buyers miss: your existing debt directly eats your housing budget. A $500/month car payment doesn't just reduce your affordability by $500 — because it counts against the 36% cap, it can reduce your max home price by far more. That's why paying down debt is often the single fastest way to afford more house.

Once the housing budget is fixed, the calculator solves for the home price whose PITI (at your rate, down payment, tax rate, and insurance) exactly fills that budget. This is the same amortization math as a standard mortgage calculator, but run in reverse.

3. Worked Example: $120k Income

Take a buyer earning $120,000/year ($10,000/month gross) with $800/month in existing debts, a $60,000 down payment, a 6.8% 30-year rate, 1.1% property tax, and $1,800/year insurance:

  1. Front-end limit: 28% × $10,000 = $2,800/month.
  2. Back-end limit: 36% × $10,000 − $800 = $3,600 − $800 = $2,800/month.
  3. Housing budget: the smaller of the two = $2,800/month.
  4. Solve for price: a PITI of $2,800 at these inputs corresponds to roughly a $409,000 home — a $349,000 loan producing about $2,275 P&I, $375 property tax, and $150 insurance per month.

Now watch what a $500/month car payment does. Back-end becomes 36% × $10,000 − $1,300 = $2,300, which now binds. The max price drops to roughly $335,000 — about $74,000 less house, purely from one car loan. This is the back-end ratio doing its quiet work.

💡 Strategic Pro Tip:
The result is a ceiling, not a target. Budget maintenance at ~1% of home value per year and buy 10–20% below your max. A bigger down payment is the fastest safe lever — it lowers the loan and, past 20%, removes PMI.

4. The Four Levers That Move Your Price

1. Down Payment

Going from 10% to 20% down does double duty: it shrinks the loan and eliminates PMI (~0.5–1% of the loan annually). On the example above, reaching 20% down raises the affordable price by tens of thousands without increasing risk. Use a closing costs calculator to make sure you can fund both the down payment and the closing bill without draining savings.

2. Existing Debt

Every dollar of minimum payment is a dollar of housing budget lost, plus it tightens the 36% cap. Before house-hunting, check your debt-to-income (DTI) ratio and attack high-rate balances — it improves both your approval odds and your max price.

3. Interest Rate

A 0.5% rate improvement adds roughly 6–7% to your affordable price. A higher credit score is the cleanest way to earn a better rate; even buying a point can pay off if you stay past the break-even. Once rates drop, a refinance break-even analysis tells you whether to lock the savings.

4. Location (Taxes & Insurance)

Property tax rates span 0.3% (Hawaii) to over 2.2% (New Jersey). Moving your search one county over can shift your affordable price by 10% or more because taxes and insurance are part of the PITI that must fit the budget.
