The MET Formula

Calories = MET x Weight (kg) x Hours

One MET = resting burn. Example: Running at 8 METs, 180 lb (81.6 kg), 30 min: 8 x 81.6 x 0.5 = 326 calories.

Scale the formula to a 140-lb (63.6 kg) woman: yoga at 3 METs x 63.6 x 1 hr = 191 cal; running 6 mph (9.8 METs) x 63.6 x 0.5 hr = 311 cal. Same workout, ~90 fewer calories than the 180-lb example — burn scales linearly with weight, which is why lighter runners must run longer (or eat less) for equal deficits. Body weight is the dominant variable in every burn estimate; intensity is second.

The Compendium of Physical Activities catalogs 800+ MET values: gardening 4.0, mopping 3.5, stair climbing 8-9, downhill skiing 6-8, sexual activity ~1.3-2.8 (the famous ~100-cal/session study). Household labor counts: an hour of vigorous cleaning at 3.5 METs burns ~286 cal for our 180-lb person — NEAT is exercise that doesn't feel like it, and it accrues without a gym membership.

Why Trackers Overestimate

Wearables overestimate calorie burn by 20-40% on average because:

  • They credit gross calories (including BMR you'd burn anyway) rather than net exercise burn.
  • Wrist heart-rate spikes from arm movement inflate estimates.
  • Algorithms assume average efficiency; trained people burn less per mile.

Rule: Discount tracker calories by 30%, and never "eat back" full exercise calories when dieting.

The Stanford 2017 verdict: across 7 popular devices, heart-rate error was modest (±5%) but energy-expenditure error ran 27-93% — the worst device nearly doubled true burn. Newer models improved, but no wrist device escapes the fundamental problem: it can't see your efficiency, body composition, or the BMR it's supposed to subtract. Treat the calorie number as entertainment with a ±30% band, and never as a meal plan.

Gross vs net — the distinction that matters: a 400-cal run includes ~75 cal of BMR you'd have burned on the couch, so the exercise netted 325. Apple's "active calories" vs "total calories" split exists for exactly this reason; most apps report gross. When dieting, budget from net burn (gross x 0.7-0.8) or you'll systematically overeat your exercise back, one "earned" snack at a time.

Exercise vs Diet for Weight Loss

Reality check: 30 min running (~400 cal) = one large muffin. You cannot outrun overeating.

  • Diet drives weight loss (easier to not eat 500 cal than burn 500).
  • Exercise preserves muscle during cuts and protects the loss afterward.
  • Combined diet + exercise beats either alone for keeping weight off long-term.
  • NEAT (daily steps) often exceeds formal exercise: 10,000 steps is about 400-500 cal.

Compensation is the silent killer: studies show ~60% of exercise calories get unconsciously compensated — extra snacks, smaller NEAT, longer couch sessions. The Hadza (Tanzanian hunter-gatherers walking miles daily) burn barely more total than sedentary Westerners: the body constrains expenditure. Exercise for health, muscle, mood, and maintenance — not as a calorie eraser. The fork creates the deficit; the feet protect it.

The maintenance data: the National Weight Control Registry's 10,000+ successful maintainers average ~1 hour of daily exercise — but they lost the weight with diet first. Diet-only losers regain fastest; exercise-only losers barely lose; combined diet + exercise wins both phases. Translation: cut with the fork, keep it off with the feet, and never confuse which tool does which job.

Maximizing Burn Per Hour

  1. Intervals beat steady-state for time efficiency (plus EPOC afterburn ~6-15% extra).
  2. Incline walking (12% grade, 3 mph) burns ~400 cal/hr with low joint stress.
  3. Compound lifts (squats, deadlifts) burn more than isolation work.
  4. Fasted vs fed makes little difference to total fat loss - train when you perform best.

Hourly burn leaderboard (180 lb): running 8 mph ~1,070; cross-country skiing ~800-900; vigorous rowing ~850; fast swimming ~700-800; boxing/sparring ~700; basketball ~650; cycling 16-19 mph ~600-700. The pattern: whole-body, high-intensity, weight-bearing wins — running beats cycling at equal effort because you're hauling your mass, not rolling it. Pick from the top of this list when time is the constraint.

Rucking — walking with a loaded pack — is the efficiency hack: 40 lb at 3.5 mph burns ~550-650/hr, rivaling running with a fraction of the joint impact. A 20-lb weighted vest adds ~15-30% to walking burn; stairs with a pack stack both (8-9 METs plus load). Military ruck standards (12 miles, 45 lb, under 3 hr) exist because nothing builds work capacity cheaper — a $50 pack turns every walk into training.
