How a Reverse Mortgage Works

A Home Equity Conversion Mortgage (HECM) lets homeowners 62 and older convert home equity into cash without monthly payments. Instead of you paying the lender, the lender pays you — as a lump sum, monthly payments, a line of credit, or a combination. The loan is repaid when you sell, move out for 12+ months, or pass away.

Available Principal = Home Value × PLF (age & rate based) − Existing Liens

The Principal Limit Factor (PLF) rises with age and falls with higher rates. A 62-year-old might access ~45% of home value; a 75-year-old closer to 60%. You must still pay property taxes, insurance, and maintenance.

Age-based principal limits follow a predictable ladder worth memorizing. At 2026 expected rates near 6.5%, a 62-year-old accesses roughly 45% of the home's value, a 70-year-old about 52%, a 75-year-old near 58%, and an 80-year-old around 64%. On a $500,000 home, each five years of waiting unlocks roughly $30,000-$40,000 more. This creates a genuine timing decision: taking the loan at 62 maximizes years of tenure payments, but waiting until 70 can increase the principal limit by $50,000+. Run both scenarios if your cash need is flexible.

Non-borrowing spouses have protections many borrowers misunderstand. If you were married at closing and your younger spouse (even under 62) is listed as a non-borrowing spouse, they may remain in the home after your death without repaying the loan - but no new draws are allowed, and they must continue paying taxes and insurance. Couples with a large age gap should model the tenure payment on the younger spouse's life expectancy and confirm the non-borrowing spouse deferral period is documented at closing, not assumed.

Costs: Higher Than a Regular Mortgage

CostTypical Amount
Upfront MIP (FHA insurance)2% of home value (max $1,209,750 value)
Origination feeUp to $6,000 (capped)
Closing costs$3,000-$5,000
Annual MIP0.5% of loan balance
Servicing/interestAccrues on balance

On a $450,000 home, upfront costs can reach $15,000-$18,000. Because interest compounds on the growing balance, the debt can double in roughly 10-12 years. This is the product's core tradeoff: convenience now, equity erosion later.

The $1,209,750 maximum claim amount creates a hard ceiling that surprises owners of expensive homes. The 2% upfront MIP and the principal limit factor both apply to the lesser of your home's appraised value or $1,209,750. Own a $2,000,000 home at 70 with a 52% PLF? Your principal limit is about $629,000 - based on the cap, not your $2,000,000 value - and you still paid the full $24,195 upfront MIP. High-value homeowners should price proprietary (non-HECM) reverse mortgages, which have no federal cap and sometimes lower upfront costs, though they lack the FHA insurance backstop.

The annual 0.5% MIP is the quiet compounder. On a growing $300,000 balance it adds $1,500 in year one, but as interest accrues and the balance climbs toward $500,000, the annual MIP approaches $2,500 - charged on top of the interest itself. Over 15 years, cumulative MIP can exceed $30,000. This is the price of the FHA guarantee that you can never owe more than the home's value. Borrowers who are certain they will sell within 5-7 years sometimes find proprietary products with no ongoing MIP cheaper overall.

Worked Example: Payout Options at 70

Scenario: Age 70, home worth $500,000, no existing mortgage, expected rate 6.5%. Principal limit ≈ $255,000.

  • Lump sum: ~$255,000 at closing (minus ~$16,000 in costs → ~$239,000 net)
  • Tenure payments: ~$1,450/month for life while living in the home
  • Line of credit: $255,000 available, with unused portion growing ~6.5%/year
  • Combo: $100,000 lump sum + $800/month tenure

The line of credit is often the smartest choice: it grows over time, you pay interest only on what you draw, and it acts as a standby emergency fund.

The line-of-credit growth feature is genuinely unique in lending: the unused portion grows at the loan's expected rate plus 0.5% MIP - roughly 6.5-7% annually in 2026 - with no market risk. A 70-year-old who opens a $255,000 line and touches none of it for 10 years has about $478,000 available at 80. Financial planners increasingly recommend opening the HECM line early as standby insurance and drawing from the portfolio first, reversing the traditional order. The growth is contractual, not projected - it is the closest thing to a guaranteed 6.5% return available to retirees.

Tenure payments look modest until you grasp mortality credits. Our $1,450/month tenure payment totals $348,000 over 20 years - far more than the $255,000 principal limit - because borrowers who die early subsidize those who live long. This pooling is exactly what makes tenure payments beat a DIY drawdown: a retiree self-managing $255,000 at 6.5% could safely draw only about $1,100/month for 20 years. The $350/month gap is the longevity insurance premium working in your favor - but only if you actually live long enough to collect it.

When It Makes Sense — and When It Doesn't

Good fit: You plan to stay in the home long-term; you need supplemental income and have limited other assets; you want to delay Social Security to age 70; you have no heirs concerned about inheritance.

Poor fit: You may move within 5 years (costs won't amortize); you need the equity for heirs; a HELOC or downsizing would solve the cash need more cheaply; you cannot reliably pay taxes and insurance (default triggers foreclosure).

Required: HUD-approved counseling before closing — a genuine consumer protection, not a formality. Bring your questions there.

Coordinating with Social Security timing is the highest-value use most borrowers miss. Consider drawing $2,000/month from a HECM line of credit from age 62 to 70 instead of claiming Social Security early. Each year you delay past full retirement age grows your benefit about 8% - delaying from 62 to 70 can roughly double the monthly check, from ~$1,800 to ~$3,400 in typical cases. The $192,000 drawn from home equity buys a $19,200/year larger inflation-protected, tax-advantaged income stream for life. Few financial maneuvers available to 62-year-olds beat that return.

Tax and insurance default - not market risk - is the number one cause of HECM foreclosures, and it is preventable. If your residual income is tight, the lender can establish a Life Expectancy Set-Aside (LESA), carving out a portion of your principal limit to pre-pay future taxes and insurance. A $40,000 LESA on a $255,000 principal limit reduces your available draws but virtually eliminates foreclosure risk. Borrowers who struggled with property tax payments before the HECM should request a fully-funded LESA at closing rather than gambling on future discipline.
