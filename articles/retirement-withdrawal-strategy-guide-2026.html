The Optimal Withdrawal Order

Conventional wisdom sequences accounts to minimize lifetime taxes:

  1. Taxable accounts first: Pay capital gains rates (0-20%) while letting tax-advantaged accounts compound.
  2. Tax-deferred (traditional IRA/401k) second: Fill up low brackets; do partial Roth conversions in gap years.
  3. Tax-free (Roth) last: Let it grow longest; no RMDs; best for legacy or late-life medical costs.
Annual Tax = Ordinary Brackets on IRA Withdrawals + Capital Gains Rates on Taxable Sales

Exception: If RMDs at 75 will shove you into higher brackets, accelerate traditional withdrawals/conversions in your 60s — "filling the bracket."

Upgrade the rigid 4% rule with guardrails. Start at $40,000 on a $1,000,000 portfolio; if a bear market drags the portfolio 20% to $800,000, trim spending 10% to $36,000; if it surges 30%, raise 10%. Research on dynamic strategies shows guardrails extend portfolio life by 5-10 years versus blind inflation adjustments — a small, temporary lifestyle flex buys a decade of solvency. Write the guardrail rules down before retirement, when you're calm, not during the crash.

The bucket strategy operationalizes the same idea: hold 2 years of spending ($80,000-$120,000) in cash and T-bills. In a 2022-style 20% drawdown, you spend from the cash bucket instead of selling stocks at the bottom — avoiding locking in $160,000 of losses on a $800,000 equity slice. Refill the bucket in up years by skimming gains. It's mental accounting, but mental accounting that prevents the single most destructive retiree behavior: panic-selling.

The 4% Rule: Tested Against History

The 4% rule (withdraw 4% initially, adjust for inflation yearly) survived nearly all 30-year historical periods for a 60/40 portfolio. But nuances matter:

  • Current valuations: At high starting valuations, some researchers suggest 3.3-3.7% for 30-year safety.
  • Flexibility beats precision: Cutting spending 10% after down years extends portfolio life more than starting at 3.5%.
  • Longer retirements: Retiring at 55 (40-year horizon) → consider 3.0-3.5%.

Example: $1.5M portfolio → $60,000/year at 4%. With $30,000 Social Security, total $90,000/year gross.

Sequence-of-returns risk dwarfs average returns. Two retirees, $1,000,000 each, identical 7% average returns over 30 years. Retiree A suffers −15% and −10% in years 1-2; Retiree B gets those same returns in years 29-30. Retiree A runs dry around year 24; Retiree B finishes with over $1,400,000. Same average, $1.4 million difference. The first five years of retirement determine the outcome more than the next twenty-five — which is why early-retirement portfolios should de-risk before the retirement date, not after.

Two refinements worth knowing. The bond tent: hold 60-70% bonds at retirement, then glide back toward 60/40 over ten years — armor when sequence risk peaks, growth later. Kitces ratcheting: if the portfolio grows 50% above its inflation-adjusted starting value, raise spending 10% permanently. Ratcheting lets you actually enjoy upside — a retiree who started at $40,000 and ratchets twice spends $48,400/year — without jeopardizing the plan's safety margin.

Watch the IRMAA cliffs when planning conversions. Medicare Part B and D premiums jump at income thresholds — and crossing one by a single dollar can cost a couple $2,000+/year in extra premiums. Because IRMAA looks back at tax returns from two years prior, a 2026 Roth conversion affects 2028 premiums. Model every conversion against the IRMAA tiers two years out; sometimes converting $5,000 less saves $2,000 in premiums — an instant 40% return on restraint.

QCDs are the retiree's best tax tool: for 2026 you can donate up to $111,000 directly from an IRA to charity starting at age 70½. A $40,000 QCD at a 24% marginal rate saves $9,600 versus withdrawing the cash and donating it (which wastes the deduction against the standard deduction). QCDs satisfy RMDs dollar-for-dollar with zero income recognition — if you're charitably inclined and 70½+, run every donation through a QCD first.

RMD Planning From Age 75

Required minimum distributions start at 75 (SECURE 2.0). RMD ≈ prior-year balance ÷ life-expectancy factor (27.4 at 75).

$2M traditional IRA at 75: first RMD ≈ $73,000 — stacked on Social Security, easily pushing a couple into the 22-24% bracket. Strategies:

  • Roth conversions in your 60s/early 70s to shrink the traditional balance
  • QCDs to satisfy RMDs charitably without income recognition
  • Delay Social Security to 70 while living on IRA withdrawals in your 60s

Avoid the April 1 double-RMD trap. Your first RMD may wait until April 1 of the year after you turn 75 — but then you must take two RMDs in that single calendar year (the delayed one plus the current year's). On a $2 million IRA that's roughly $150,000 of bunched ordinary income, likely pushing you into a higher bracket and higher IRMAA tiers. In most cases, taking the first RMD in the actual year you turn 75 produces the lower lifetime tax bill.

Two more RMD nuances: inherited IRAs — non-spouse heirs face the 10-year rule, so a $500,000 inherited IRA forces roughly $50,000+/year of taxable withdrawals on top of your own income. Roth conversions before death convert that burden into tax-free inheritance. And the still-working exception: if you're employed past 75 and don't own 5%+ of the company, your current employer's 401(k) is exempt from RMDs until you retire — a quiet way to defer taxes on that balance for years.
