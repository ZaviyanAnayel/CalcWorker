A "cup" isn't universal, and the spread is bigger than most cooks realize. US customary cup = 236.6 ml, metric cup = 250 ml, imperial (older UK/Canada) cup = 284 ml — a 20% spread end to end. A recipe calling for 2 cups of stock is 473 ml (US) versus 500 ml (metric): in soup it doesn't matter, in baking ratios it can. Tablespoons diverge too: US 14.8 ml, metric 15 ml, traditional UK 17.7 ml. When a British recipe says "4 tablespoons of golden syrup," that's 71 ml versus 59 ml American — a 20% sweetness swing from the same spoon count.

Fluid ounces bridge the systems but carry their own split: 1 US fl oz = 29.57 ml, 1 imperial fl oz = 28.41 ml — and a US pint is 16 fl oz (473 ml) while an imperial pint is 20 fl oz (568 ml). The "pint's a pound" mnemonic only works in the US (a pint of water ≈ 1.04 lb). For precision work, convert everything to milliliters or grams once: 1 cup = 240 ml is the practical kitchen standard (matching the US nutrition-label cup), and scale from there.

A cup of flour vs a cup of honey differ by 215g - volume-to-weight is ingredient-specific. For baking, a $15 kitchen scale beats every conversion chart.

Extend the chart with baking staples: 1 cup chocolate chips = 170g, powdered sugar = 120g, brown sugar (packed) = 220g, chopped nuts = 120g, uncooked rice = 185g, water or milk = 236g. The flour trap is the worst offender: scooping directly from the bag packs 150g+ per cup versus 125g spooned-and-leveled — a 20% flour excess that turns tender cake into brick. That single technique difference explains most "I followed the recipe exactly!" failures, and no conversion chart can fix it — only the scale can.

Professionals skip cups entirely with baker's percentages: flour is always 100%, and every other ingredient is a percent of flour weight. A lean bread dough: 500g flour (100%), 350g water (70%), 10g salt (2%), 5g yeast (1%). Scaling becomes trivial — 750g flour means 525g water — and the hydration percentage (70% here) predicts crumb structure precisely. Convert your five most-baked recipes to grams once; the $15 scale pays for itself within the first month of consistent results.

Home ovens lie by ±25°F, and a $8 oven thermometer is the highest-ROI kitchen tool after the scale. If your oven runs 25°F hot, "350°F" is really 375°F: cookies spread too fast and bottoms burn while centers stay raw. Convection runs hotter still: reduce recipe temps by 25°F (350°F → 325°F) or cut time 20-25%, never both at once. Dark pans absorb more radiant heat — drop another 25°F versus shiny aluminum, or the bottoms scorch before the tops set.

Candy and sugar work demand exact Fahrenheit: soft ball 235°F (fudge), firm ball 245°F (caramel), hard ball 250°F (nougat), soft crack 270°F (taffy), hard crack 300°F (brittle, lollipops) — a 5°F miss changes the texture category entirely. Altitude shifts boiling point ~2°F per 1,000 ft: at 5,000 ft (Denver), water boils at 203°F, so subtract ~7°F from every sugar target or the syrup overcooks. A $15 digital probe thermometer ends the guesswork for both oven and candy.

Scaling Recipes

  • Double: Multiply everything x2 - but keep leavening at x1.75 (too much makes bitter).
  • Halve: Divide by 2; eggs: beat and measure half by volume (1 egg ~ 3 tbsp).
  • Pan scaling: 9" round to 8" round: area ratio (64/81) = use 80% of batter.
  • Spices: Scale at 75% then adjust - they don't scale linearly.
  • Cooking time: Larger batches need more time, not proportionally - check doneness, not the clock.

Leavening scales sub-linearly: double the batter but multiply baking powder/soda by only 1.75x — a full 2x leaves a bitter, soapy aftertaste and can collapse the crumb. Salt scales at ~1.5x when doubling; spices at 1.5x then adjust to taste, since volatile oils concentrate unpredictably. Eggs: one large egg ≈ 3 tablespoons beaten — for half an egg, beat one egg and measure out 1.5 tablespoons. Yeast is the exception: it scales nearly linearly with flour, but proofing time stays roughly constant since fermentation is driven by temperature, not quantity.

Time doesn't double when quantity does. A doubled cake batter in the same pan needs ~50% more time, not 100% — and often a 25°F lower temperature so the center catches up without burning the edges. Better: split into two pans and keep the original time. For roasts, time scales with thickness, not weight: a 6-lb roast that's twice as thick as a 3-lb one needs ~40-50% more time, but a 6-lb roast that's merely longer needs barely more. Measure internal temp with the $15 probe — never trust the clock.
