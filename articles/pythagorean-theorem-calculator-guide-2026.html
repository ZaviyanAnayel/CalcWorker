The Theorem

a^2 + b^2 = c^2 (c = hypotenuse, opposite the right angle)

Find hypotenuse: legs 6 and 8: c = sqrt(36 + 64) = sqrt(100) = 10.

Find a leg: hypotenuse 13, leg 5: a = sqrt(169 - 25) = sqrt(144) = 12.

The converse is the inspector's version of the theorem: if a^2 + b^2 = c^2, the triangle is right. Sides 7, 24, 25 satisfy 49 + 576 = 625 = 25^2, so the angle between the 7 and 24 sides is exactly 90 degrees. But 5, 7, 9 give 25 + 49 = 74 versus 81 - not equal, so the triangle is not right. Testing squareness from measurements alone is how the converse earns its keep.

Special right triangles skip the square roots. Isosceles right: legs x give hypotenuse x times sqrt(2) - legs of 10 mean a hypotenuse of about 14.14. The 30-60-90 triangle: hypotenuse 2x, short leg x, long leg x times sqrt(3) - a hypotenuse of 10 gives legs 5 and about 8.66. Recognizing these ratios turns a radical computation into mental arithmetic.

Euclid's formula manufactures triples on demand: pick integers m > n, and (m^2 - n^2, 2mn, m^2 + n^2) is always a triple. With m = 3, n = 2: (9 - 4, 12, 9 + 4) = (5, 12, 13). With m = 4, n = 1: (15, 8, 17). There are infinitely many primitive triples, and this 2,300-year-old recipe generates every one of them - contest problems about "find a triple containing 20" start here.

Before reaching for the theorem, check whether a triple is just a scaled classic. Sides 15, 20, 25 are 5 x (3, 4, 5); 30, 40, 50 are 10 x (3, 4, 5); 9, 12, 15 are 3 x (3, 4, 5). Divide all three sides by their GCD first - if the reduced triple is familiar, the hypotenuse (or missing leg) is immediate. Scaling is the most underused shortcut in right-triangle problems.

Distance Formula Connection

Distance = sqrt((x2-x1)^2 + (y2-y1)^2)

Example: From (2, 3) to (8, 11): dx = 6, dy = 8, distance = sqrt(36+64) = 10 (a 6-8-10 triangle!).

GPS, mapping, and game physics all run on this - it's Pythagoras in coordinate form.

The distance formula stacks into three dimensions without new ideas: distance = sqrt(dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2). From (1, 2, 3) to (4, 6, 9): dx = 3, dy = 4, dz = 6, so distance = sqrt(9 + 16 + 36) = sqrt(61), about 7.81. It is Pythagoras applied twice - once across the floor, once up the vertical - and it powers everything from molecular modeling to drone navigation.

Walking distance versus straight-line distance is Pythagoras in city form. Six blocks east plus eight blocks north is 14 blocks on foot, but the straight-line shortcut is exactly 10 - the 6-8-10 triangle. Delivery apps charge road distance; a future drone service would charge the 10. Whenever two routes differ, the gap between them is usually a hypotenuse.

Construction: The 3-4-5 Trick

To check a corner is square: mark 3 ft along one wall, 4 ft along the other; the diagonal must be exactly 5 ft.

  • Ladder safety: 20-ft ladder, base 5 ft out: reaches sqrt(400-25) = 19.4 ft up.
  • TV sizes: A "55-inch TV" is the diagonal - with 16:9 ratio, width ~48", height ~27".
  • Diagonal bracing: Rectangular frame 3x4 ft needs a 5-ft brace.

Scale the 3-4-5 trick to the job. Small woodworking: mark 3, 4, 5 inches. Room layouts: 6, 8, 10 feet - easy tape-measure numbers. Building foundations: 30, 40, 50 feet keeps the ratio while staying readable on a long tape. The ratio is the tool; the unit size just needs to be convenient at the scale you are working.

Pair the trick with the diagonal test for full verification. A 20 x 15 foot foundation outline is truly rectangular only if both diagonals measure exactly 25 feet - equal diagonals prove it is a rectangle, and the 3-4-5 check proves the corners are 90 degrees. Use diagonals to confirm the shape, 3-4-5 to confirm the squareness: together they certify a foundation before concrete is poured.
