Markup Formula

Price = Cost x (1 + Markup %) | Markup % = (Price - Cost) / Cost

$40 cost, 60% markup: 40 x 1.60 = $64.

Reverse: Selling at $64 with $40 cost: (64-40)/40 = 60% markup (37.5% margin).

Markup on raw materials alone is how busy shops go broke. A $40 product with $12 of allocated overhead (rent, labor, insurance per unit) has a true cost of $52; a 60% markup then prices it at $83.20, not $64. Overhead allocation feels like guesswork until you do it - then it feels like the difference between profit and a hobby.

Price for the sale you plan to run. Want a 40% margin after a planned 25% discount? The list price must satisfy (0.75L - C)/(0.75L) = 0.40, giving L = C/0.45 - a 122.2% markup. On a $40 cost: list $88.89, sale $66.67, margin (66.67-40)/66.67 = 40%. Retailers who markup for the sticker price and then discount are donating margin.

Don't forget payment processing eats margin after markup: 2.9% + $0.30 per transaction. On a $64 sale ($40 cost, 60% markup), the $2.16 fee cuts your $24 gross profit to $21.84 — effective margin drops from 37.5% to 34.1%. On small tickets it's worse: a $10 sale at 100% markup ($5 cost) nets $5 − $0.59 = $4.41, shaving over 10% off the margin. Price with net-of-fees math, or set minimum order values to protect small sales.

Margin approaches 100% asymptotically as markup grows - it never arrives. Each extra markup point buys less margin than the last, which is why luxury goods need enormous markups to move the margin needle: 400% markup still leaves 20% of the price as cost.

Work backward from the margin target. Want 45% margin? Markup = 0.45/0.55 = 81.8%, so a $40 cost prices at $72.73; check: 32.73/72.73 = 45%. Starting from the target margin and deriving the markup beats guessing a markup and hoping the margin works out.

Pricing Strategy Layers

  • Cost-plus: Simple, but ignores what customers will pay.
  • Keystone (100%): Retail standard - covers overhead, discounts, shrinkage.
  • Psychological: $64 vs $59.99 - charm pricing lifts conversion measurably.
  • Tiered: Good/better/best - most buyers pick middle, raising average margin.
  • Loss leader: Below-cost anchor drives traffic; profit on attachments.

Charm pricing has a measurable cost. Dropping $64 to $59.99 cuts margin from 37.5% to 33.3% ((59.99-40)/59.99), so unit volume must rise about 12.5% just to hold profit flat. Published tests show 5-10% conversion lifts from .99 pricing - sometimes enough, sometimes not. Run the break-even on the price change per product instead of applying charm pricing as superstition.

Bundles trade margin rate for margin dollars. Two products costing $40 and $30 bundled at $99 carry margin (99-70)/99 = 29.3% - below either item's keystone margin, but average order value jumps. The bundle's job is not efficiency; it is moving more units per transaction and clearing slow inventory alongside winners.

Worked Retail Example

Boutique candle: Cost $8 (wax, jar, labor).

  • Keystone price: $16 (100% markup, 50% margin)
  • After 20% sale: $12.80 - still 37.5% margin
  • Wholesale at $12 (50% markup): retailer keystones to $24
  • Your margin at wholesale: (12-8)/12 = 33.3%

Price for your channel's discount reality, not the sticker.

Shrinkage is a hidden cost - price it in. Five percent theft and damage on the $16 candle makes the effective cost $8/0.95 = $8.42, so the true margin is (16-8.42)/16 = 47.4%, not 50%. Retailers with 3% shrinkage who price at "50% margin" are really at 48.4%. Either cut shrink or markup for it; ignoring it is a donation.

MAP pricing defends the brand's margin structure. Wholesale at $12 with a $24 minimum advertised price: a discounter at $19.99 still earns 40% margin ((19.99-12)/19.99) but trains customers to wait for discounts, eroding every other retailer's full-price sales. Enforce MAP consistently - the margin you protect is your product's perceived value, not just one retailer's profit.
