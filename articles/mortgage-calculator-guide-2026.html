1. What a Mortgage Payment Really Is

Most homebuyers quote a monthly number from a lender's pre-approval letter without ever seeing what's inside it. A mortgage payment is not one number — it is a stack of four (sometimes five) obligations bundled together. The industry shorthand is PITI: Principal, Interest, property Taxes, and home Insurance. When your down payment is below 20%, a fifth item, PMI (private mortgage insurance), is layered on top.

Why does this matter in 2026? Because the average 30-year fixed rate has settled near 6.8%, every quarter-point on the note rate moves your payment far more than buyers expect. On a $350,000 loan, a 0.25% rate difference is roughly $58 per month — over $20,000 across the life of the loan. Understanding the components lets you see exactly where that money goes, and where you can claw some of it back.

This guide walks through the exact math, gives a fully worked example you can replicate by hand, and flags the costs most people forget. Use our free mortgage calculator to run your own numbers alongside.

2. The PITI Formula, Broken Down

The heart of a mortgage is the amortized loan payment, calculated with the standard fixed-rate formula:

M = P × [ r(1 + r)^n ] ÷ [ (1 + r)^n − 1 ]

M = monthly principal & interest | P = loan amount
r = monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12) | n = total payments (years × 12)

Then the full payment is assembled on top:

PITI = M + (annual property tax ÷ 12) + (annual homeowners insurance ÷ 12) + PMI

Each Component, Explained

  • Principal: the portion that pays down what you actually borrowed.
  • Interest: the lender's charge, front-loaded. In the first years of a 30-year loan, over 70% of each payment is interest — that's why early extra payments are so powerful.
  • Taxes: county/municipal property tax, typically 0.5%–2.0% of the home's assessed value per year depending on state, collected through escrow.
  • Insurance: homeowners hazard insurance, typically $1,000–$2,500/year for a standard single-family home.
  • PMI: applies when your loan-to-value (LTV) ratio exceeds 80%. It costs roughly 0.5%–1.0% of the loan amount per year and protects the lender, not you.

The beauty of the amortization formula is that it holds a payment constant while the principal/interest split shifts every month. Payment one is almost all interest; payment 300 is almost all principal. Our calculator shows this year by year in the amortization schedule.

3. Step-by-Step Worked Example

Let's calculate a realistic 2026 purchase — a $450,000 home with 10% down, because many buyers can't reach 20%:

  1. Loan amount: $450,000 − $45,000 (10% down) = $405,000 borrowed.
  2. Monthly rate: 6.75% ÷ 12 = 0.005625. Payments: 30 × 12 = 360.
  3. Principal & interest: M = 405,000 × [0.005625 × (1.005625)^360] ÷ [(1.005625)^360 − 1] ≈ $2,627/month.
  4. Escrow: property tax $5,400/year → $450/month; insurance $1,800/year → $150/month.
  5. PMI: LTV is 90%, so PMI at 0.65% of the loan = $405,000 × 0.0065 ÷ 12 ≈ $219/month.
  6. Total PITI: $2,627 + $450 + $150 + $219 = $3,446/month.

Over 30 years this buyer pays roughly $540,700 in interest alone on top of the $405,000 principal. Two insights jump out. First, PMI costs $219/month yet builds zero equity — eliminating it early is pure savings. Second, the same home at 20% down would skip PMI entirely and cut the interest bill by roughly $67,500, because the loan is smaller to begin with.

💡 Strategic Pro Tip:
You can request PMI cancellation at 80% LTV under the Homeowners Protection Act — you don't have to wait for the automatic 78% trigger. If your home appreciates or you make extra payments, track your LTV and file the request in writing; it's often worth $150–$250/month.

4. PMI, Escrow & Hidden Costs

The most common miscalculation is treating the principal-and-interest figure as "the payment." Lenders quote P&I because it's flattering, but escrow and PMI can add 30–40% on top. Always demand the PITI number before comparing offers.

Escrow Cushion & Annual Reanalysis

Lenders are legally allowed to hold a cushion of up to two months of escrow payments (RESPA rules). When property taxes or insurance premiums rise — as they did broadly through the mid-2020s — your servicer sends an annual escrow analysis that can produce a surprise payment jump. Review that statement every year; billing errors on tax or insurance lines are common and reversible.

Points, APR, and the Note Rate

The note rate is what computes your payment; the APR folds in origination fees and points, so it's the true cost of credit. When two lenders quote "the same rate," compare APRs — a "6.5% with 1 point" offer may cost more than a "6.7% with zero points" offer depending on how long you stay. Don't Forget the Upfront Bill

Your monthly PITI is only half the picture. Closing costs run 2–5% of the purchase price — that's $9,000–$22,500 on this example home — plus moving and immediate repairs. Run our closing costs calculator before you commit, and keep an emergency fund separate from your down payment.

6. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

In 2026 the average 30-year fixed rate sits near 6.8%. Borrowers with credit scores above 740 and a 20%+ down payment can often lock rates 0.3–0.5% below average, while sub-680 scores and low down payments push rates noticeably higher.

PITI is Principal, Interest, property Taxes, and home Insurance. Lenders roll annual property tax and homeowners insurance into a monthly escrow payment so the full obligation is collected automatically. When your down payment is under 20%, PMI (private mortgage insurance) is added as a fifth component.

PMI is required on conventional loans with under 20% down and typically costs 0.5%–1.0% of the loan annually. It is automatically cancelled under the Homeowners Protection Act once your loan-to-value ratio reaches 78%, but you may request cancellation at 80% LTV, which usually happens within 7–10 years on a 30-year loan.

A 15-year mortgage carries a lower rate and can save $200,000+ in interest, but its monthly principal-and-interest payment runs roughly 40–50% higher. A 30-year loan maximizes cash-flow flexibility. Many advisors recommend taking the 30-year and making extra principal payments only when your budget allows.
