The Three Core Formulas

What is X% of Y? (X/100) x Y
X is what % of Y? (X/Y) x 100
X is Y% of what? X / (Y/100)

Examples: 20% of 85 = 17. 17 is what % of 85? 20%. 17 is 20% of what? 85. Master these three and every percentage problem reduces to one of them.

The reverse-percentage move trips up even confident students: a jacket on sale for $81 after 25% off did not originally cost $101.25 - you must divide by what remains, not add back the discount. Original = 81 / 0.75 = $108. Same logic for raises: a $97,200 salary after an 8% raise means the old salary was 97,200 / 1.08 = $90,000. The rule is always divide by (1 +/- rate as a decimal). Subtracting 25% of $81 gives $60.75, which is wrong because the 25% was taken off $108, not $81.

Chained percentages compound rather than add. A town of 50,000 grows 12% then 8%: 50,000 x 1.12 x 1.08 = 60,480, a total increase of 20.96% - not 20%. Savings accounts work the same way: $5,000 at 6% for 3 years becomes 5,000 x 1.06^3 = $5,955.08, earning $955.08 rather than the $900 simple interest would suggest. Whenever a percent applies to a new, already-changed base, multiply the factors instead of adding the rates.

Increase and Decrease

% Change = (New - Old) / Old x 100

Salary: $65,000 to $71,500: (71,500-65,000)/65,000 x 100 = 10% raise.

Discount: $120 jacket at 25% off: 120 x 0.75 = $90. Stack a 10% coupon: 90 x 0.90 = $81 (not 35% off - percentages multiply, they don't add).

Here is a surprise: the order of a discount and a sales tax does not change the final price. A $100 item with 20% off and 8% tax costs 100 x 0.80 x 1.08 = $86.40 whether the cashier discounts first or taxes first, because multiplication commutes. Many shoppers insist on "discount before tax" as if it saves money - it does not, though stores are legally required in most states to compute tax on the discounted price anyway. The math simply does not care about the order.

Stacked discounts are where retailers profit from fuzzy math. A "30% off plus an extra 20% off" sign is not 50% off: 0.70 x 0.80 = 0.56, so you pay 56% of the price - a 44% discount. On a $200 jacket that means 200 x 0.70 = $140, then 140 x 0.80 = $112. A true 50%-off sale would price it at $100, so that "extra 20%" is worth only $28, not $40. Always multiply the keep-fractions (0.70 and 0.80), never add the discount rates.

Common Percentage Traps

  • Percentage points vs percent: Rate rises 5% to 7% = +2 points, but a 40% relative increase.
  • 50% off then 50% more: $100 -50% = $50, +50% = $75. You lost 25%.
  • Success rate illusions: "99% effective" still fails 1 in 100 - over thousands of users, that's many failures.
  • Base confusion: A 10% pay cut then 10% raise: 100 - 10% = 90, +10% = 99. You never recover.

Loss recovery is the cruelest percentage asymmetry in investing. A $10,000 portfolio that drops 50% to $5,000 needs a 100% gain - not 50% - just to break even. The general rule: after a p% loss, you need p/(100-p) x 100% to recover. A 20% loss needs a 25% gain, a 33% loss needs 50%, and a 90% loss needs 900%. This is why risk management beats return-chasing: avoiding big losses matters far more than the arithmetic of gains suggests.

Periodic rates deceive the same way. A credit card charging 1% per month is not a 12% annual rate - the effective annual rate is 1.01^12 - 1 = 12.68%. At the typical 1.5% monthly store-card rate, the true annual cost is 1.015^12 - 1 = 19.56%, not 18%. Always convert a periodic rate with (1 + r)^n - 1 before comparing offers. Lenders quote the smaller nominal number precisely because the effective number looks worse.

Mental Math Shortcuts

  1. 10%: Move the decimal one place ($64 - 10% = $6.40).
  2. 5%: Half of 10% ($3.20).
  3. 15%: 10% + 5% ($9.60 tip).
  4. 20%: Divide by 5.
  5. 25%: Divide by 4.

Combine: 18% of $64 = 10% ($6.40) + 5% ($3.20) + 3x1% ($1.92) = $11.52.

The 1%-anchor method handles any percentage without memorized tricks. Find 1% first by moving the decimal two places, then multiply: 23% of $240 means 1% = $2.40, so 23 x $2.40 = $55.20. Fractional percents work too: 7.5% of $240 is 7 x $2.40 + 0.5 x $2.40 = $16.80 + $1.20 = $18.00. One anchor, one multiplication, and every tip, tax, or commission calculation becomes mechanical.
