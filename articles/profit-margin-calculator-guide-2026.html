The Three Margins

MarginFormulaWhat It Shows
Gross(Revenue - COGS) / RevenueProduction profitability
OperatingOperating Income / RevenueCore business efficiency
NetNet Income / RevenueBottom-line reality

Example: Revenue $500K, COGS $200K, operating expenses $180K, taxes/interest $30K.

  • Gross: (500-200)/500 = 60%
  • Operating: (500-200-180)/500 = 24%
  • Net: 90/500 = 18%

Margin compression tells the story revenue hides. Year one: $500K revenue, $200K COGS - 60% gross margin. Year two: $550K revenue but $264K COGS - gross margin falls to 52%. Revenue grew 10% while profitability decayed 8 points; a costs problem is wearing a growth costume. Track margin percentages quarter to quarter - dollars flatter, margins confess.

Contribution margin is the margin that pays the fixed bills: price minus variable cost. A $50 product with $30 in variable costs contributes $20 (40%) toward rent, salaries, and profit. Gross margin includes allocated overhead; contribution margin is the incremental truth of one more sale - and it is the number the break-even formula actually needs.

Watch operating leverage: businesses with high fixed costs see margins swing violently with revenue. A company with $500,000 in fixed costs and 60% gross margin breaks even at $833,333 revenue; at $1,000,000 revenue the operating margin is 10%, but at $1,200,000 it jumps to 20% — profits doubled on 20% more sales. This cuts both ways: a 10% revenue dip can erase half the profit. Low-fixed-cost businesses (freelancers, digital products) have steadier margins but less explosive upside.

Markup vs Margin: Stop Confusing Them

Margin = (Price - Cost) / Price | Markup = (Price - Cost) / Cost

$60 cost, $100 price: Margin = 40/100 = 40%. Markup = 40/60 = 66.7%.

Converting: Margin = Markup/(1+Markup). A 50% markup = 33.3% margin. Retailers often target keystone (100% markup = 50% margin).

The reverse conversion runs: Markup = Margin / (1 - Margin). A 40% margin target means 0.4/0.6 = 66.7% markup on cost. Buyers negotiate in margin, suppliers quote in markup - fluency both directions prevents the classic error of granting a "40% discount" that wipes out a 40% margin (it does not; it wipes out far more).

A 100% margin is mathematically impossible - it would require zero cost. Doubling a $40 cost to an $80 price is a 100% markup but only a 50% margin. Anyone promising "100% profit margins" is either quoting markup or innumerate; the margin asymptote means each extra markup point buys less margin than the last.

Compare against your industry - a 6% net margin is excellent in grocery, terrible in software.

The Rule of 40 judges SaaS companies on one number: growth rate plus profit margin should clear 40. A company growing 30% at 15% margin scores 45 - healthy. One growing 50% at negative 20% margin scores 30 - flashing amber. Investors use it because it prices the growth-versus-profitability tradeoff without a spreadsheet.

Direction beats level. A restaurant climbing from 4% to 7% net margin over three years signals strong management even while sitting mid-pack for the industry. A software firm sliding from 25% to 14% is the real red flag despite still looking "above average." Benchmark the snapshot, but manage the trend.

Improving Margins

  1. Raise prices - a 5% price increase at 20% margin boosts profit ~25%.
  2. Cut COGS - renegotiate suppliers, reduce waste.
  3. Upsell mix - shift sales toward high-margin products.
  4. Automate - reduce labor cost per unit.
  5. Kill losers - drop products/customers with negative contribution margin.

Pricing leverage is asymmetric: a 10% price increase beats a 10% cost cut. Take a $100 product costing $70 (30% margin). Raise the price to $110: margin becomes 40/110 = 36.4% and profit per unit jumps $10 - a 33% profit increase from a 10% price move. Cost cuts of equal size rarely exist; price increases of equal size often do. Test price before cutting costs.

Some revenue deserves firing. One hundred clients, and the bottom ten lose $500 each - $5,000 of annual drag. Dropping them lifts profit $5,000 with zero new sales, and frees capacity for profitable work. Negative-contribution customers are charity disguised as revenue; the margin math says let them be someone else's charity.
