Multi-year guaranteed annuities (MYGAs) are the annuity world's quiet workhorse in 2026. A MYGA works like a CD - you deposit a lump sum, it grows at a guaranteed rate (recent offerings near 5.0-5.5% for 5-year terms), and gains compound tax-deferred until withdrawal. Unlike CDs, there is no bank, no FDIC insurance (state guaranty associations back them instead, typically to $250,000-$500,000 depending on the state), and early withdrawals trigger surrender charges plus a 10% IRS penalty before 59½. For retirees in high tax brackets parking cash for 3-7 years, the tax deferral alone can beat a CD's headline rate.

Qualified Longevity Annuity Contracts (QLACs) solve the RMD problem elegantly. SECURE 2.0 raised the QLAC limit to $200,000 (indexed), letting you move up to $200,000 from your IRA into a deferred annuity that starts payments as late as age 85 - and that $200,000 is excluded from required minimum distribution calculations until payments begin. A 73-year-old with a $1,000,000 IRA who QLACs $200,000 cuts annual RMDs by roughly $7,500, lowering taxable income and potentially Medicare IRMAA surcharges. It is longevity insurance plus tax planning in one instrument.

Payout Math: What $250,000 Buys

Scenario: 65-year-old buys an immediate annuity with $250,000 in 2026.

  • Life only: ~$1,550/month ($18,600/year) — 7.4% payout rate, payments stop at death
  • Life + 10-year certain: ~$1,450/month — heirs get payments for at least 10 years
  • Joint life (spouse): ~$1,300/month — continues until both pass

The payout rate looks like a 7.4% return, but part of each payment is return of principal. The true internal rate of return depends on longevity — live to 90 and it is excellent; die at 70 and it is poor (without a period-certain guarantee).

Payouts are brutally age-sensitive because mortality credits do the heavy lifting. That same $250,000 premium buying at age 60 generates roughly $1,300/month; at 65, ~$1,550; at 70, ~$1,750; at 75, over $2,000. Each five years of waiting buys about $200/month more for life - an increase no safe investment can replicate, because the insurer is pricing in fewer expected payment years. This creates a real optimization: a healthy 65-year-old who can fund early retirement from portfolio draws should consider deferring the SPIA purchase to 70 or 75, buying dramatically more monthly income per premium dollar.

Inflation riders illustrate the cruelest tradeoff in the product. Adding a 2% annual payment increase to our $1,550/month SPIA cuts the starting payment to roughly $1,170-$1,240 - a 20-25% haircut - and the higher payment does not catch up to the flat version until about year 12-14. With 2026 inflation running above target, the rider tempts - but the breakeven demands living into your late 70s just to break even, and the insurer prices the rider profitably. Alternative: buy the flat SPIA and invest the $350/month difference; historically that portfolio hedge has beaten the rider's terms.

Fees That Erode Variable Annuities

Variable and indexed annuities carry layered fees:

  • Mortality & expense: ~1.25%/year
  • Subaccount fees: 0.5-1.0%/year
  • Riders (income guarantees): 0.5-1.5%/year
  • Surrender charges: 7-10% declining over 7-10 years

Total drag of 2.5-3.5%/year is common — meaning the market must return that much before you earn a dime. Fixed and immediate annuities have no such drag, which is why fee-conscious planners prefer them.

Surrender schedules punish early exits with precision. A typical variable annuity charges 7-9% declining over 7-10 years: surrendering a $250,000 contract in year 2 at an 8% charge costs $20,000 - more than most years of market gains. Even "bonus" annuities that credit 5% upfront bury the bonus in longer, steeper surrender periods. Before signing, ask for the surrender schedule in writing and compute the dollar cost of exiting in years 1, 3, and 5. If you cannot comfortably lock the money up for the full schedule, you are buying the wrong product - full stop.

The 10% annual free-withdrawal provision is your pressure valve. Nearly all deferred annuities allow withdrawing up to 10% of the account value yearly without surrender charges - $25,000/year on a $250,000 contract. Strategic uses: supplementing income in down market years without selling portfolio assets, funding the wait until a deferred SPIA purchase, or staged exits from a high-fee variable annuity (10% per year over several years avoids the surrender charge entirely). Know this provision exists before you need it; agents rarely lead with it.

When Annuities Make Sense

  1. Longevity insurance: You are healthy at 65 with family history of long life — a SPIA hedges outliving your money.
  2. Pension substitute: No employer pension? A SPIA manufactures one.
  3. Behavioral guardrail: You will not panic-sell an annuity in a crash.
  4. Avoid when: you need liquidity, have ample assets, or are sold a high-fee variable annuity for "growth" — buy index funds instead.

The 4% rule versus SPIA comparison reveals what mortality credits are worth. A $250,000 portfolio drawn at 4% yields about $833/month with inflation adjustments and preserves principal for heirs. The SPIA pays $1,550/month - 86% more - but consumes the principal. The $717/month gap is the market price of pooling longevity risk with thousands of other annuitants. Neither is universally better: the portfolio wins if you die at 72 (heirs keep $230,000+), the SPIA wins decisively if you live to 92 ($1,550/month for 27 years totals $502,000). Your family longevity history is the single best input to this decision.

Laddering SPIA purchases hedges the inflation risk that single-purchase annuities ignore. Instead of annuitizing $300,000 at 65, buy $150,000 at 65 (~$930/month), another $100,000 at 70 (~$700/month at the better age-70 rate), and hold $50,000 for 75. Each rung buys at a higher payout rate, and the later purchases are funded by portfolio growth in the interim. You sacrifice some early income for a rising payment stream that better matches retirement's increasing costs - particularly healthcare, which inflates faster than everything else.
