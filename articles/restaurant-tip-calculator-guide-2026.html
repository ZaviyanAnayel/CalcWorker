Tip creep is measurable in payment data: the average US sit-down tip sits around 19-20% in 2025-26, up from ~15% in the 1990s — and tablet prompts now default to 20/25/30%, anchoring higher than the old 15/18/20% register. The counter-trend is tip fatigue: counter-service prompts (coffee shops, food trucks) get tipped only ~30-40% of the time, averaging 12-15% when they do. Watch for fee confusion too: California and several states now require service charges to be disclosed upfront — a "5% kitchen appreciation fee" is not a tip and doesn't reach your server.

Delivery tipping has its own economics. 15-20% on the subtotal is standard, but flat $4-$6 minimums matter more on small orders — a $12 order at 20% is $2.40, below the effort threshold most drivers expect. Bad weather and long distances deserve +5%. And check for double-dipping: some apps add a "service fee" (kept by the platform) alongside the tip prompt — the fee doesn't go to the driver, so don't mentally subtract it from the tip.

Tip Math Shortcuts

Tip = Bill x Rate | Total = Bill x (1 + Rate)

$84 dinner, 20%: 10% = $8.40, double = $16.80. Total: $100.80.

15%: 10% + half (5%): $8.40 + $4.20 = $12.60.

Tip on pre-tax: Proper etiquette tips the pre-tax subtotal. On $84 + $7 tax: 20% of $84 = $16.80 (not 20% of $91).

Pre-tax versus post-tax tipping carries a real dollar gap. $84 subtotal + 8.75% tax ($7.35) = $91.35 bill. Twenty percent pre-tax = $16.80; twenty percent post-tax = $18.27 — a $1.47 difference per dinner, $76/year for weekly diners, paid to no one's benefit (servers are taxed the same either way). Proper etiquette is pre-tax, and most POS systems compute suggested tips on the post-tax total — mentally subtract the tax line first. On a $200 business dinner at 9% tax, the gap is $3.60 per meal.

Mental-math shortcuts beyond 10%-double: 18% = 20% minus 10% of the tip (20% of $84 = $16.80, minus $1.68 = $15.12). Fifteen percent = 10% + half again ($8.40 + $4.20 = $12.60). Twenty-two percent = 20% + 10% of the tip ($16.80 + $1.68 = $18.48). Round the bill first for speed: $84 → $85 makes 20% an instant $17.00. For bar tabs, $1/beer or $2/cocktail beats percentage math — a $14 cocktail at 20% is $2.80 anyway, so the flat rule lands in the right zone.

Splitting Bills Fairly

$240 bill, 4 people, 20% tip, 8% tax:

  • Subtotal each: $60
  • Tax each: $4.80
  • Tip each: $12
  • Each pays: $76.80

Uneven orders: Each person: (their items + proportional tax) x 1.20. Apps split the math, but the formula keeps it fair when someone had the lobster.

Parties of 6+ usually trigger 18-20% auto-gratuity — check before adding more, because double-tipping the auto-gratuity is the most common split-bill error. On a $480 check with 18% auto-gratuity ($86.40), an extra "20% tip" adds $96 — the server collects 38% and your group overpaid by nearly a hundred dollars. If service was exceptional, add 2-5% on top, not another full 20%. Policies vary (some restaurants trigger at 8+, some only on weekends) and print on the menu — read it when the group sits down, not when the bill arrives.

Shared-item math keeps friendships intact. A $36 appetizer split four ways = $9 each; add each person's items plus their share of shared plates, apply proportional tax, then ×1.20 for tip. The lobster problem from the section, solved: on a $240 bill where one diner had a $65 entrée and the others $40, equal splits punish the modest orderers by $6+ each. Apps like Splitwise automate it, but the formula — (personal items + shared share) × 1.08 tax × 1.20 tip — settles any dispute in about 60 seconds.

International Tipping

  • Japan/Korea: Tipping can offend - don't.
  • Europe: Service often included; round up or 5-10% for great service.
  • UK: 10-12.5% if service charge isn't added.
  • Middle East: 10-15% common in tourist areas.
  • Latin America: 10-15% where not included.

When in doubt, ask locals or check the bill for "service compris/included."

More country rules worth memorizing: China — no tipping, and offering can genuinely confuse; Australia/New Zealand — 10% for exceptional service only, never expected; Brazil — 10% "serviço" is legally included on the bill, extra is optional; South Africa — 10-15% standard; Thailand — loose change or 10% in tourist spots; India — 10% in restaurants, often already included as a service charge in cities. Cruise ships auto-charge $14-$18/person/day in gratuities — those "included" tips are real and prepaid, so extra tipping is genuinely optional rather than expected.

"Service compris" doesn't always mean what Americans assume. In France, 15% service is included by law, but locals still leave €1-2 or round up for good service — leaving nothing is fine, leaving 20% marks you as a tourist. In the UK, check for the 12.5% discretionary service charge: it's removable on request, and adding a cash 5% on top of an included 12.5% totals 17.5% — generous but deliberate. Rule of thumb abroad: if a service charge is printed on the bill, you're done; if not, 5-10% cash says thank you without the American 20% overshoot.
