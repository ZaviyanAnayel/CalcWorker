Consolidation Options Compared

Origination fees quietly inflate the "11% loan" you were quoted. Many personal-loan lenders charge 1-8% upfront, deducted from the disbursement: a $25,000 loan with a 5% fee deposits only $23,750 but you repay $25,000 plus interest. Your true APR is closer to 12.5%, not the advertised 11%. Worse, if you needed the full $25,000 to pay off cards, you must borrow ~$26,300 to net $25,000 after the fee. Always ask for the APR including fees (lenders must disclose it under TILA) and the net disbursement amount - then compute savings on the true numbers, not the headline rate.

Consolidation reshapes your credit profile in ways that help mortgage applications. Converting $25,000 of revolving card debt into an installment loan typically drops your revolving utilization from, say, 80% to near zero - often lifting scores 30-60 points within two billing cycles, since utilization is 30% of your FICO score. Mortgage underwriters also prefer installment debt's fixed payoff date over revolving minimums. If homeownership is 12+ months out, consolidating first and letting the score recover can earn you a better mortgage rate tier worth far more than the consolidation's interest savings.

Worked Example: $25,000 Across 4 Cards

Current: $25,000 at average 23% APR, minimum payments ~$625/month → payoff in 68 months, total interest $17,400.

Consolidation loan: $25,000 at 11% for 48 months → payment $645/month, total interest $5,960.

Result: Nearly identical monthly payment, but you finish 20 months sooner and save $11,440 in interest. The fixed end date is the real win — minimum payments are designed to keep you in debt.

Test the no-consolidation path before committing, because the math is closer than lenders admit. Take the same $25,000 at 23%: instead of minimum payments, pay a fixed $745/month using the avalanche method (minimums on three cards, everything extra on the highest-rate card). Payoff lands around month 42 with roughly $6,200 in total interest - versus $5,960 with the consolidation loan. The DIY path costs only $240 more in interest and requires no application, no origination fee, and no hard inquiry. Consolidation's real edge is behavioral - one fixed payment versus the discipline of self-directed avalanche - not mathematical.

When rates are mixed, avalanche-plus-partial-consolidation often wins. Suppose the $25,000 spans cards at 29%, 24%, 19%, and 15%. Consolidating everything at 11% helps - but consolidating only the 29% and 24% cards ($14,000) at 11% while avalanching the 19% and 15% cards yourself produces lower total interest than the full consolidation, because you avoid origination fees on the portion you can handle. Price consolidation per-card, not per-total: refinance only the tranches where the rate improvement exceeds the fee drag.

The #1 Consolidation Mistake

Consolidation fails when borrowers run the cards back up. Studies suggest a majority of consolidators carry new card balances within two years — ending with the consolidation loan plus fresh card debt.

Prevention protocol:

  1. Before consolidating, fix the cash-flow gap that created the debt (budget, income, or spending).
  2. After consolidation, keep ONE card for monthly bills (paid in full), freeze or close the rest.
  3. Automate the consolidation payment for payday.
  4. Build a $1,000 starter emergency fund first so surprises do not go back on cards.

The re-accumulation statistics are grim enough to plan around explicitly: industry studies consistently find a majority of debt consolidators carry significant new card balances within two years. The mechanism is psychological - freed-up credit limits feel like found money, and the consolidation loan's lower payment creates false budget slack. The prevention that actually works is structural, not willpower-based: remove saved card numbers from every online store, delete shopping apps, keep exactly one card (autopay-in-full) for recurring bills, and set a calendar alert for 90 days post-consolidation to audit balances. Environment design beats discipline.

Decide deliberately which cards to close and which to keep open. Closing your oldest card shortens your credit history length (15% of your FICO score) and reduces total available credit, spiking utilization - a double hit. The optimal setup: keep the two oldest cards open with a tiny recurring charge (a streaming subscription on autopay), and close or sock-drawer the newest store cards with annual fees or low limits. Never close a card within 6 months of a mortgage application; the score volatility is not worth the tidiness.

When Consolidation Is Wrong

  • Tiny rate improvement: 23% → 19% barely helps; attack with avalanche instead.
  • Secured with your home: Turning unsecured card debt into HELOC debt puts your house at risk — only for disciplined borrowers.
  • Nearly done anyway: If payoff is under 12 months, fees may exceed savings.
  • Underlying overspending: Consolidation without behavior change is just rearranging deck chairs.

Nonprofit debt management plans (DMPs) are the overlooked alternative when your credit cannot qualify for 11%. Agencies like NFCC members negotiate directly with your card issuers, typically reducing rates to 6-8% and consolidating everything into one monthly payment to the agency - with no new loan, no hard inquiry spike, and no origination fee. On $25,000, a DMP at 7% over 48 months costs about $3,760 in interest: better than the 11% personal loan's $5,960. The tradeoff is a notation on your credit report and 3-5 years of closed accounts, but for sub-660 scores it is often the only mathematically sound path.

Debt settlement is the option to understand so you can reject it properly. Settlement companies instruct you to stop paying while they negotiate lump-sum payoffs at 40-60% of balances - which works on paper but triggers collections, lawsuits, trashed credit (100+ point drops), and taxes: forgiven debt over $600 counts as taxable income. On $25,000, "settling" for $12,000 might cost $3,000+ in fees, $5,000 in taxes, and years of credit damage. Settlement is a last resort before bankruptcy, not a consolidation alternative - anyone pitching it as routine is selling, not advising.
