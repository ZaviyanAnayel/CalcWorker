VA Loan Benefits: Why It Beats Conventional

VA loans, backed by the Department of Veterans Affairs, offer terms no conventional loan matches: zero down payment required, no private mortgage insurance ever, competitive rates, and no prepayment penalties. The VA guarantees a portion of the loan, which is why lenders can offer these terms.

Eligibility covers veterans, active-duty service members (90+ continuous days), National Guard/Reservists (6 years), and surviving spouses of service members who died in service. You will need a Certificate of Eligibility (COE), obtainable online through the VA portal.

Loan Amount Financed = Purchase Price + Funding Fee (if rolled in)

The three-way cost comparison makes the VA advantage concrete. On a $400,000 purchase: a VA borrower puts $0 down, pays a $8,600 funding fee (usually financed), and owes no PMI - monthly P&I around $2,590 at 6.25%. An FHA borrower puts 3.5% down ($14,000), pays a 1.75% upfront MIP ($6,755) plus 0.55% annual MIP (~$176/month for the life of the loan). A conventional borrower with 3% down pays roughly $194/month in PMI until 20% equity. Over seven years, the VA borrower typically saves $13,000-$18,000 versus FHA - the funding fee is a one-time cost while MIP and PMI bleed monthly.

Getting your Certificate of Eligibility is faster than most expect. Veterans can pull it in minutes through the VA's eBenefits portal or VA.gov; lenders can also retrieve it directly through the VA's web system with your authorization, often within one business day. You will need your DD-214 (or statement of service if active duty), and surviving spouses need VA Form 26-1817. Start this on day one of house hunting - a missing COE is the most common preventable delay in VA purchases.

2026 VA Funding Fee Tables

The funding fee replaces mortgage insurance. It can be paid cash or rolled into the loan. Veterans with service-connected disabilities are exempt.

On a $400,000 first-use purchase with 0% down, the fee is $8,600 — usually financed, making the loan $408,600. Compare that to PMI on a conventional 0%-down loan (which does not exist) or 3%-down loan: PMI at ~0.6%/year would cost $2,300+/year until 20% equity.

The subsequent-use fee jump is the detail that burns second-time VA buyers. Using the benefit again with 0% down costs 3.30% - on a $450,000 purchase that is $14,850 financed into the loan. But put just 5% down ($22,500) and the fee falls to 1.50%, or $6,413 on the $427,500 loan - a savings of $8,437. That $22,500 down payment effectively earns a 37% instant return through fee reduction alone, before counting the smaller loan balance. If you are reusing entitlement, pricing the 5%-down option is mandatory, not optional.

Disability exemptions deserve emphasis because billions in fees go unclaimed. Any service-connected disability rating - even 10% - zeroes out the funding fee entirely. On our $425,000 example, that is $9,137.50 kept in your pocket. Better still, if your disability claim is pending at closing and approved later with an effective date before closing, the VA refunds the fee retroactively - but you must apply for the refund; it is not automatic. Veterans with pending claims should calendar a follow-up with their lender six months after closing.

Worked Example: Full Monthly Payment

Scenario: Veteran buying a $425,000 home, 0% down, first use, 30-year fixed at 6.25%. Funding fee 2.15% = $9,137.50, financed → loan amount $434,137.50.

  • Principal & interest: $2,672/month
  • Property tax (1.1%): $390/month
  • Homeowner's insurance: $150/month
  • PMI: $0
  • Total PITI: ~$3,212/month

A conventional buyer with 5% down on the same home would pay roughly $3,350/month including $180/month PMI — and needed $21,250 cash at closing versus the veteran's $0 (plus closing costs).

Entitlement note: Full entitlement (no loan limit in most counties since 2020) applies if you have never used a VA loan or have repaid one in full. Partial entitlement remains if you still hold a VA loan.

Entitlement math determines how much house you can buy with $0 down, and it trips up buyers who still hold a prior VA loan. With full entitlement (never used, or prior loan fully repaid), there is no county limit in 2026. With partial entitlement, your maximum is 25% of the conforming limit minus entitlement already used. Example: county limit $832,750 × 25% = $208,187 maximum guaranty. If a prior VA loan used $90,000 of guaranty, you have $118,187 remaining - supporting a new $0-down loan up to about $472,750. Your lender's VA specialist should run this calculation before you make an offer.

Do not overlook the VA's more forgiving credit profile either. While the VA sets no minimum credit score, most lenders overlay 620 - yet VA loans consistently show more flexible debt-to-income treatment than conventional, with approvals above 50% DTI possible when residual income is strong. Residual income - money left after all obligations, varying by region and family size - is the VA's signature underwriting metric. A veteran with a 640 score and strong residual income often gets approved where a conventional lender says no.

VA Loan Limits and Property Rules

  1. Occupancy: Must be your primary residence within 60 days of closing. No investment properties.
  2. Appraisal: VA appraisals include Minimum Property Requirements (safe water, sound roof, no lead paint hazards) — stricter than conventional.
  3. Assumable: VA loans are assumable by qualified buyers, a huge selling advantage if rates rise.
  4. Second use: You can reuse the benefit; the funding fee rises to 3.3% at 0% down unless you put 5%+ down.
  5. 2026 county limits: With full entitlement there is effectively no cap; with partial entitlement the conforming limit ($832,750 in most counties for 2026) applies.

Assumability is the VA loan's hidden superpower in a high-rate market. A 2021-vintage VA loan at 3.0% can be assumed by a qualified buyer in 2026 when market rates sit near 6.5%. On a $400,000 balance, the assumable payment is about $1,686/month versus $2,528 for a new loan - a $842 monthly advantage that lets sellers command premium prices. The catch: the seller's entitlement stays tied to the loan until the assuming buyer is a veteran who substitutes their own entitlement. Non-veteran assumptions are allowed but leave the seller unable to reuse their full benefit.

The appraisal's Minimum Property Requirements end more deals than any other VA feature, so preview them. Peeling paint on pre-1978 homes, missing handrails, broken windows, and non-functional utilities all trigger mandatory repairs before closing - the VA will not waive them. In competitive markets, sellers often reject VA offers fearing repair demands. Counter this by getting a pre-inspection, budgeting $2,000-$5,000 for likely MPR fixes, and offering to cover them in your contract terms.
