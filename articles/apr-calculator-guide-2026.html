APR vs Interest Rate vs APY

These three get confused constantly:

TermWhat It MeasuresIncludes Fees?
Interest rateCost of borrowing the principalNo
APRTotal yearly cost of the loanYes
APYYearly return with compoundingN/A (savings)

A loan at 6.5% interest with $4,000 in fees on $400,000 might show 6.62% APR. APR is the only apples-to-apples comparison number — federal law (Truth in Lending Act) requires lenders to disclose it.

APR's blind spot is what it excludes, and the exclusions are large. Third-party charges like appraisal fees, title insurance, recording fees, and escrow reserves are generally not finance charges under TILA - so a mortgage with $8,000 in total closing costs might only count $4,000 in its APR calculation. Two lenders with identical 6.85% APRs can differ by thousands in true out-of-pocket costs. APR standardizes the lender's charges, not the transaction's cost. Always pair APR comparison with a line-by-line Loan Estimate review of Section A (origination charges) specifically.

Credit card interest accrues through the daily periodic rate, and APR is the input. A 24.99% APR divides by 365 to a daily rate of about 0.0685%, applied to your average daily balance. This is why timing matters: a $5,000 purchase made the day after your statement closes accrues ~25 days of daily interest before the next due date (~$85), while the same purchase made two days before closing accrues almost nothing before the grace period discussion begins. APR ÷ 365 is the number your balance actually feels.

How APR Is Computed

APR spreads all finance charges over the loan term as an annualized rate:

APR ≈ (Total Finance Charges ÷ Loan Amount) ÷ Years × Adjustment Factor

Example: $20,000 auto loan, 5-year term, $1,000 origination fee, 7% note rate. Total finance charges ≈ $3,761 interest + $1,000 fee = $4,761. The APR comes out near 7.95% — almost a full point above the note rate because of the fee.

Key insight: fees inflate APR more on short loans. That same $1,000 fee on a 3-year loan pushes APR to ~8.6%; on a 7-year loan, only ~7.7%.

Mortgage APRs carry a built-in distortion most borrowers never adjust for: the calculation assumes you keep the loan the full 30 years. But the median homeowner sells or refinances around year 7-10. Those $4,000 in points that added ~0.25% to your APR were priced over 360 months; spread over the 84 months you actually kept the loan, their true annualized cost is roughly four times higher. If your realistic holding period is under 10 years, mentally double the APR impact of all upfront fees - or better, compare loans on total cost over your expected horizon, not on APR.

Buydown points distort APR in the opposite direction from what buyers expect. Paying 2 points ($8,000 on $400,000) to cut your rate 0.5% raises your APR by about 0.25% - making the loan look worse on paper - while genuinely lowering your payment. APR treats prepaid interest as a cost, which it is, but it cannot tell you whether the tradeoff was smart. This is why APR is a fee-comparison tool, not a decision tool: a higher APR with points can still be the right choice for a 15-year holder, and a lower APR can be wrong for a 3-year holder.

APR Traps to Watch

  • Adjustable-rate APRs assume the rate never changes — fiction for ARMs. Compare margins and caps instead.
  • 0% APR offers exclude the transfer fee from the "0%" — a 5% fee on 12 months equals ~9% effective APR.
  • Payday loan APRs of 400%+ look absurd because APR annualizes a 2-week fee — but the dollar cost is what matters there.
  • Mortgage APR vs note rate: A bigger gap means higher fees. Two lenders at 6.75% note rate with APRs of 6.85% vs 7.10% — the second charges much more in fees.

Promotional credit card APRs need blended-rate math. A card offering 0% for 12 months then 24.99% is often compared against a flat 18% card as if the choice were 0% versus 18%. But if you carry a $6,000 balance for 24 months, the promo card costs roughly $750 in year-two interest (on the declining balance) - a blended 24-month APR near 11%. The flat 18% card costs about $1,170. The promo card still wins, but the honest comparison is 11% versus 18%, not 0% versus 18%. Always blend the promo and go-to rates over your realistic payoff timeline.

Rent-to-own and installment lenders live in APR territory above 100%, where dollar framing exposes the absurdity. A $1,200 sofa at "only $35/week for 78 weeks" totals $2,730 - an effective APR near 150%. The weekly framing is deliberate: $35 sounds trivial, 150% sounds obscene, and they describe the same contract. Whenever a lender quotes payments without a rate, compute the implied APR yourself: (total paid - cash price) / cash price, annualized. If they will not give you the numbers to compute it, walk away.

Using APR to Choose

  1. For identical terms, lowest APR wins — always.
  2. For different terms, compare total cost (payment × months + fees), since APR assumes you keep the loan full term.
  3. If you will refinance or sell in 3 years, weight upfront fees more heavily than APR suggests — low-fee, slightly-higher-rate loans often win short-term.
  4. On credit cards, APR only matters if you carry a balance. Pay in full and the APR is irrelevant.

Small APR gaps matter enormously at mortgage scale - develop a tolerance rule. On a $400,000 30-year loan, each 0.125% of rate equals about $30/month, or roughly $10,800 over the full term ($2,500 over a 7-year holding period). So an APR gap of 0.125% between two lenders is worth real negotiation effort, while a 0.02% gap is noise. Use this to allocate your shopping energy: get three solid quotes and negotiate the best two against each other, rather than collecting eight quotes to chase a 0.03% improvement that saves $7/month.

Term length changes what APR tells you. Term length changes what APR tells you: fees amortized over 180 months instead of 360 produce a larger APR spread, not smaller. A $4,000 fee package might add 0.15% to a 30-year APR but 0.28% to a 15-year APR. Never compare a 15-year APR against a 30-year APR directly - the shorter loan always looks fee-heavier. Compare 15-year APRs only against other 15-year APRs, where the fee amortization is apples-to-apples.
