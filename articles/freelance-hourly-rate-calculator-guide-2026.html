The Rate Formula

Hourly Rate = (Salary Goal + Expenses + Taxes + Profit) / Billable Hours

Example: Want $100K salary-equivalent.

  • Target income: $100,000
  • Business expenses: $8,000 (software, insurance, home office)
  • Self-employment tax buffer: ~$15,000
  • Total needed: $123,000
  • Billable hours: 1,000/yr (50% of 2,000 - admin, marketing, gaps)
  • Rate: $123/hour

Most beginners use 2,000 hours and forget taxes - that's how $50/hr freelancers go broke.

The 2,080-hour year is a myth for freelancers. Four weeks off leaves 1,920 working hours; at 50% utilization that is 960 billable hours. The $123,000 target divided by 960 gives $128/hour - $5 above the naive math. Vacations, sick days, and holidays are not free; your rate is where clients fund them.

Employees get retirement matches and paid leave - freelancers must price their own safety net. Add 10% profit margin and 15% retirement savings to the $123,000 target: $153,750 over 1,000 billable hours = $154/hour. Skipping this step is why so many freelancers earn "good money" yet retire on nothing.

New freelancers: use 500-800 billable hours in year one. Established: 1,000-1,200.

Measure utilization monthly: billable hours divided by total hours worked. Billing 25 of 40 hours is 62.5%. Lifting utilization from 50% to 60% on a 1,000-hour base adds 200 billable hours x $123 = $24,600 a year - a raise you grant yourself without a single awkward client conversation. Track it like revenue, because it is.

Price the invisible work or it bankrupts you. Ten hours a week on marketing, proposals, and admin across 50 weeks is 500 unpaid hours - at $123/hour, $61,500 of free labor yearly. That "quick proposal" and the networking coffee are baked into your rate. Every hour you cannot bill is an hour your billable rate must carry.

Pricing Strategies

  • Hourly: Simple, but punishes efficiency - faster work = less pay.
  • Project-based: Price on value/deliverables. $5K website > 40 hrs x $100.
  • Retainer: Monthly recurring for ongoing work - the holy grail of stability.
  • Value-based: Price on client ROI. A landing page generating $200K = worth far more than hours.
  • Day rate: Common in consulting - 8-hour blocks at a premium.

Value pricing beats hourly when your work moves revenue. A redesign lifting a client's sales from $500K to $650K is worth far more than the 60 hours it took. Price at 10% of the $150K gain - $15,000 - instead of 60 x $123 = $7,380. Hourly billing punishes your speed; value pricing charges for the outcome the client actually bought.

Productized retainers trade per-hour upside for pipeline sanity. "$2,500/month for unlimited design requests, pause anytime": ten clients means $25,000 MRR - $300K a year with zero proposals and zero scope fights. The hourly equivalent is irrelevant; what you bought is predictable income and a waitlist.

Raising Rates

  1. Raise 10-15% yearly minimum - inflation plus skill growth.
  2. Grandfather existing clients 3-6 months, then move them.
  3. New clients always get the new rate immediately.
  4. When booked out 4+ weeks: raise rates until demand normalizes.
  5. Never apologize - state the rate confidently; negotiate scope, not price.

Frame the increase in dollars, not percentages. Moving $100 to $115 an hour for a 20-hour-a-month client is "$300 more per month" - which sounds smaller than "a 15% increase" and keeps the conversation on scope rather than sticker shock. State the new rate plainly, negotiate what is included, never apologize for the number itself.

Protect the whale. If one client is 40% of your income, raise everyone else first and reprice the anchor client only after two replacements are signed. Concentration risk turns a routine rate hike into a potential layoff - diversify before you renegotiate the client you cannot afford to lose.
