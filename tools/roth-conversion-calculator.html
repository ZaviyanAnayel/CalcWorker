Frequently Asked Questions

When does a Roth conversion make sense?

A conversion makes sense when your current tax bracket is lower than your expected tax bracket in retirement, or if you want to eliminate mandatory RMDs and leave tax-free wealth to heirs.

How do I pay the taxes on a Roth conversion?

It is best practice to pay the upfront tax with non-retirement cash savings so the entire converted sum remains in the Roth to compound tax-free.

Is there an income limit on Roth conversions?

No. Anyone can convert Traditional IRA assets to a Roth IRA regardless of income, which forms the basis of the popular 'Backdoor Roth' strategy.

What is the 5-year rule for Roth conversions?

Each conversion has its own 5-year holding clock before converted principal can be withdrawn penalty-free if you are under age 59½.

🔄 Roth IRA Conversion Economics: Tax Bracket Arbitrage & Break-Even Horizons

A Roth Conversion transfers funds from a pre-tax traditional 401(k) or traditional IRA into a post-tax Roth IRA under Internal Revenue Code § 408A(d)(3). The converted sum is added to your ordinary income in the year of conversion and taxed at current marginal brackets, in exchange for permanent tax-free compounding thereafter.

Executing a Roth conversion represents a strategic wager on future tax policy: paying taxes today is financially advantageous if you expect your future marginal tax bracket in retirement to be equal to or higher than your current tax rate, or if you wish to eliminate massive future Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs).

⚡ Roth Conversion Execution Protocol

  • Always Pay Conversion Taxes From Non-Retirement Cash: Never withhold taxes from the conversion amount itself; using retirement capital to pay conversion taxes incurs a 10% early withdrawal penalty if under age 59½ and destroys compounding leverage.
  • Fill Up Low Marginal Brackets Sequentially: Convert only enough pre-tax funds each year to reach the top boundary of your current marginal tax bracket (e.g. topping off the 22% or 24% bracket) without spilling into higher tiers.
  • Watch Out for IRMAA Medicare Surcharges: Large Roth conversions spike Modified Adjusted Gross Income, which can trigger Medicare Part B and Part D Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount (IRMAA) premium surcharges two years later.
